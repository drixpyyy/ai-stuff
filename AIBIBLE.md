## BOOK I — GENESIS: THE CONCEPTUAL FOUNDATIONS (1940s-1950s)

### The Birth of Artificial Intelligence

**Alan Turing (1950):** Published "Computing Machinery and Intelligence," proposing the Turing Test as a measure of machine intelligence. Turing argued that machines could think if they could convincingly imitate human conversation. This paper established the philosophical foundation for AI—the question was no longer *if* machines could think, but *how* we would know if they did.

**Key Concepts:** The Imitation Game, the Church-Turing thesis, the concept of universal computation.

**Claude Shannon (1948):** Published "A Mathematical Theory of Communication," establishing information theory. Shannon showed that information could be quantified in bits, laying the groundwork for how AI systems would later represent and process data.

**Key Concept:** Information entropy as a measure of uncertainty.

**The Dartmouth Conference (1956):** John McCarthy, Marvin Minsky, Nathaniel Rochester, and Claude Shannon organized a workshop that coined the term "Artificial Intelligence." They believed that every aspect of learning and intelligence could be so precisely described that a machine could simulate it. This optimistic vision defined the field's early decades.

**Key Figure:** John McCarthy later created LISP, the primary AI programming language for decades.

**McCulloch-Pitts Neuron (1943):** Warren McCulloch and Walter Pitts proposed a simplified mathematical model of a biological neuron. It took binary inputs, applied weights, and fired if the sum exceeded a threshold. This was the first artificial neuron—the ancestor of every neural network that followed.

**Mathematical Form:** y = f(∑wᵢxᵢ - θ), where f is a step function.

**The Perceptron (1958):** Frank Rosenblatt built on McCulloch-Pitts to create the Perceptron, an early neural network that could learn weights through training. It used a simple learning rule: adjust weights based on errors. The Perceptron could learn to classify simple patterns, but it had fundamental limitations.

**Key Limitation:** The Perceptron could only learn linearly separable patterns—it could not solve XOR, a simple logical operation. This limitation later caused the first AI winter.

## BOOK II — EXODUS: THE RISE AND FALL OF EARLY AI (1960s-1980s)

### The Logic-Based AI Era

**Symbolic AI:** Early AI researchers believed intelligence could be created through explicit rules and logic. Systems like DENDRAL (1965) used expert-defined rules to solve chemistry problems. These systems were brittle—they failed when encountering situations outside their pre-programmed rules.

**Key Concept:** The "physical symbol system hypothesis"—the belief that intelligence is about manipulating symbols according to rules.

**ELIZA (1966):** Joseph Weizenbaum created a simple chatbot that simulated a Rogerian psychotherapist. ELIZA used pattern matching and substitution rules—if you said "I feel X," it responded "Why do you feel X?" It was shockingly effective at fooling users into thinking they were talking to a real therapist, despite having zero understanding.

**Why ELIZA Mattered:** It demonstrated that humans are predisposed to anthropomorphize machines. Even minimal linguistic competence triggered powerful illusions of intelligence.

**Technique:** Pattern matching with scripted responses. ELIZA had no model of language—just clever text replacement.

**The Frame Problem (1969):** Philosopher John McCarthy identified a fundamental challenge: how does an AI know which facts are relevant to a situation and which can be ignored? This problem haunted symbolic AI—explicitly encoding every possible context was impossible.

**Shakey the Robot (1969):** The first mobile robot with AI reasoning capabilities. Shakey could navigate environments, plan actions, and reason about its world. It used STRIPS (Stanford Research Institute Problem Solver), a planning algorithm that defined problems in terms of states and actions.

**Key Innovation:** Combining perception, planning, and action in a physical system.

**The First AI Winter (1974-1980):** Government funding dried up because AI systems failed to meet expectations. Early systems were brittle, narrowly focused, and couldn't scale. Researchers had overpromised and underdelivered.

**Reasons for the Winter:** Limitations of symbolic AI (handling exceptions required endless rules), poor performance on perception tasks (computer vision was decades away), and unrealistic optimism about timelines.

### The Energy-Based Revival — The Forgotten Bridge to Attention (1980s)

**Hopfield Networks (1982):** John Hopfield (Princeton), building on William Little (1974) and the Ising model of magnetism, published *“Neural networks and physical systems with emergent collective computational abilities”* (PNAS 1982). A single layer of binary threshold units with symmetric bidirectional weights `w_ij = w_ji`. Hopfield defined an **energy function** `E = -½ Σ w_ij s_i s_j - Σ θ_i s_i`. Dynamics = asynchronous updates that *always* decrease energy until a stable attractor. Key properties: **associative (content-addressable) memory** — clamp a noisy/partial pattern and the network settles to the nearest stored memory; distributed representation; fault tolerance. Capacity ≈ 0.14·N patterns (later ~30% of N with refinements). Nobel Prize in Physics 2024 (shared with Geoffrey Hinton) *for Hopfield networks* — the first time energy-based associative memory was formalized. Without Hopfield, the concept that a network can *store and retrieve* patterns by minimizing an energy is missing.

**Boltzmann Machines (1985):** Geoffrey Hinton, Terry Sejnowski & David Ackley created a **stochastic generalization** of Hopfield nets — add hidden units, make updates probabilistic via the **Boltzmann distribution** `p(s) ∝ exp(-E(s)/kT)`, with update `p=1/(1+exp(-ΔE/T))`. The network can *escape* local minima (simulated annealing) and *learn* a distribution over visible units, not just a deterministic attractor. Training via Contrastive Divergence (later) approximates `⟨v_i h_j⟩_data - ⟨v_i h_j⟩_model`. Invented for the Feb/Mar 1983 talk Hinton had to give on annealing in Hopfield nets — he “had to design a learning algorithm for the talk” (Hinton, 1995 interview). The fully-connected BM was too slow, but **Restricted Boltzmann Machines (RBMs, Smolensky’s Harmonium 1986, refined by Hinton 2006)** removed intra-layer links — bipartite visible↔hidden — making layer-wise training tractable. RBMs became the *pre-training* engine for Deep Belief Nets (§VI) and the formal birth of **Energy-Based Models (EBMs)**: models that learn by assigning *low energy* to correct inputs and *high energy* to incorrect ones (LeCun et al., *A Tutorial on Energy-Based Learning*, 2006).

**Why This Was the Attention Bridge (The Audit’s Key Insight):** Modern Transformer attention `softmax(QKᵀ/√d)V` is *mathematically equivalent* to a **Modern Hopfield Network with continuous states** (Ramsauer et al., *Hopfield Networks is All You Need*, ICLR 2021/2020). Q = query pattern, K = stored patterns, V = associated values — exactly associative memory retrieval, but differentiable and exponentially higher capacity. In other words: Transformers can be viewed as **differentiable associative memories** that retrieve values by minimizing an energy-like softmax. Without Hopfield → Boltzmann → RBM → EBM, the story of *why* attention works as memory is incomplete. Yann LeCun’s 2006 EBM tutorial is the formal link.

**Backpropagation Rediscovered (1986):** David Rumelhart, Geoffrey Hinton, and Ronald Williams popularized backpropagation—a method for training multi-layer neural networks. By propagating errors backward through the network, they could adjust weights in hidden layers, enabling neural networks to learn complex patterns.

**Why It Was Revolutionary:** Backpropagation allowed networks with multiple layers to learn—this was the foundation of "deep learning." The Perceptron's limitation (XOR) was solved by adding hidden layers.

**Mathematical Key:** Gradient descent—adjust weights in the direction that minimizes error.

**The Connectionist Movement:** Researchers argued that intelligence emerges from simple processing units (neurons) connected in networks. This was a direct challenge to symbolic AI—connectionists believed that learning and adaptation, not explicit rules, were the path to intelligence.

**Key Figure:** Geoffrey Hinton became the central figure of the connectionist revival. His persistence through decades of skepticism earned him the title "Godfather of AI."

## BOOK III — NUMBERS: THE STATISTICAL REVOLUTION (1990s-2000s)

### From Rules to Probability

**The Shift in Paradigm:** AI researchers increasingly abandoned explicit rules in favor of statistical methods. Instead of programming intelligence, they would build systems that learned patterns from data.

**Markov Chains:** A mathematical system that models sequences of states where the probability of each state depends only on the previous state. Markov chains became fundamental to language modeling.

**How It Works:** Given the current word, predict the next word based solely on that word's transition probabilities. Example: If you're at "the," predict the next word based on which words historically followed "the."

**Limitation:** Markov chains have "short memory"—they can't capture long-range dependencies because they only consider the immediate previous state.

**n-gram Models:** An extension of Markov chains to sequences of n words. Instead of looking at just one previous word, n-gram models look at n-1 previous words.

**Example:** A trigram model predicts the next word based on the previous two words. "I love" might predict "you" if that bigram appeared frequently in training data.

**Limitation:** n-gram models suffer from data sparsity—as n increases, the number of possible n-grams explodes, and most never appear in training data even if they're valid.

**Hidden Markov Models (HMMs):** Applied Markov chains to situations where states are hidden—you observe outputs but not the underlying states. HMMs became the dominant approach for speech recognition.

**Example:** In speech recognition, the underlying words are hidden states, and the acoustic signals are observations. The HMM models the probability of word sequences given the acoustic signals.

**Probabilistic Models:** Markov chains, HMMs, and other probabilistic models treated language as a statistical phenomenon—the most probable sequence was the "correct" interpretation. This worked surprisingly well but was fundamentally limited.

## BOOK IV — RUTH: THE FIRST CHATBOTS (1990s-2000s)

### Pattern Matching and Retrieval

**A.L.I.C.E. (1995):** Richard Wallace created an improved chatbot that used AIML (Artificial Intelligence Markup Language)—an XML language for defining pattern-matching rules. A.L.I.C.E. won the Loebner Prize three times (2000, 2001, 2004), fooling judges into thinking it was human.

**How It Worked:** A.L.I.C.E. matched input patterns to pre-defined templates. It had thousands of rules, but no real understanding—it was essentially an elaborate search and replace.

**Limitation:** A.L.I.C.E. was brittle—it failed when encountering unfamiliar patterns. It couldn't learn, adapt, or maintain context beyond a few exchanges.

**Key Technique:** Recursive pattern matching—patterns could refer to other patterns, creating a network of responses.

**Cleverbot (2008):** Created by Rollo Carpenter, Cleverbot represented a significant shift in chatbot design. Instead of relying on hand-coded rules, Cleverbot learned from a massive corpus of human conversations—thousands of hours of human chat interactions.

**How It Worked:** Cleverbot stored past conversations and retrieved responses based on the closest match to the user's input. It used context matching and statistical methods to select responses, rather than pre-programmed scripts.

**Key Technique:** Retrieval-based response generation—find similar conversations in the database and use their responses.

**Why Cleverbot Felt Smarter:** Because it was trained on real human conversations, Cleverbot's responses were more varied and natural than A.L.I.C.E.'s scripted replies. It could handle a wider range of topics because it had actually seen those topics discussed by humans.

**Limitation:** Cleverbot was essentially doing sophisticated copy-paste—it wasn't generating new responses, just retrieving existing ones. It had no real understanding of language, just pattern matching at a larger scale.

**The "Cleverbot Effect":** When Cleverbot was put on the internet, many users believed they were talking to a human. It captured about 60% of the emotional impact of a real conversation, despite having zero intelligence.

**Eviebot (2012):** Eviebot was an evolution of Cleverbot—a web-based chatbot with a 3D avatar (a young woman named Evie). It used the same retrieval-based mechanism but with a more polished interface.

**How Eviebot Worked:** Like Cleverbot, Eviebot searched its conversation database for similar inputs and retrieved corresponding responses. It could use social media (Twitter) to expand its knowledge.

**Key Limitation:** No real understanding—Eviebot would sometimes produce hilariously inappropriate or nonsensical responses because it was just matching patterns, not understanding meaning.

## BOOK V — DEUTERONOMY: THE RISE OF STATISTICAL MACHINE LEARNING (2000s-2010s)

### The Data-Driven Era

**Support Vector Machines (SVMs):** A powerful classification algorithm that finds the optimal boundary between classes. SVMs became the dominant machine learning technique in the early 2000s, especially for text classification.

**How They Work:** SVMs map data into high-dimensional space and find the hyperplane that best separates classes. The "support vectors" are the data points closest to the boundary.

**Advantage:** SVMs could handle high-dimensional data and performed well on many tasks with minimal parameter tuning.

**Limitation:** SVMs were "shallow"—they couldn't learn hierarchical features. Performance plateaued with more data.

**Random Forests:** An ensemble learning method that builds many decision trees and aggregates their predictions. Random forests were widely used because they worked well "out of the box" with minimal tuning.

**How They Work:** Each decision tree is trained on a random subset of data and features. The forest's prediction is the average (for regression) or majority vote (for classification) of all trees.

**Advantage:** Robust to overfitting, handles mixed data types, provides feature importance scores.

**Limitation:** Still shallow learning—couldn't capture deep patterns.

**The Rise of Graphical Models:** Bayesian networks, Markov random fields, and other graphical models treated AI as a problem of probabilistic inference. They were powerful but required careful manual specification of structure.

**Key Concept:** Probabilistic graphical models combine probability theory with graph theory to represent complex dependencies.

**Expectation-Maximization (EM):** An algorithm for finding maximum likelihood estimates when data has hidden variables. EM became essential for unsupervised learning.

**How It Works:** Alternates between estimating hidden variables (E-step) and updating parameters (M-step), iteratively improving the solution.

## BOOK VI — JOB: THE EARLY NEURAL REVIVAL (2000s-2012)

### Backpropagation Returns

**The Hinton Group (University of Toronto):** Geoffrey Hinton, along with students like Alex Krizhevsky and Ilya Sutskever, kept the neural network torch burning through the 2000s when most of the field had abandoned them.

**Key Insight:** Hinton believed that deep (multi-layer) neural networks could learn hierarchical features if trained properly—the problem wasn't the architecture, but the algorithms and data.

### The Hardware Lottery No One Talks About — CUDA (2007)

**CUDA (NVIDIA, 2007 → 2006 release, 2007 ecosystem):** Compute Unified Device Architecture gave developers C-like access to GPU parallel cores. Before CUDA, GPUs were graphics-only; after, they were *GPGPU* machines. Between 2006-2012 a small cult (Andrew Ng, Dan Ciresan, etc.) used CUDA to train nets 20-50× faster than CPUs. Every major framework later targeted CUDA first; competitor chips had to emulate it. **Without CUDA, AlexNet never happens.** From 2012 onward, Nvidia added **cuDNN (2014)** and **Tensor Cores (2017 Volta: 4×4 FP16 MAC per cycle)** — the “secret” speed behind 2012-2026 scaling. By late 2010s, 35% MFU on trillion-param training was considered best-in-class *because* CUDA kernels were that optimized; vendor lock-in became the moat.

**Restricted Boltzmann Machines (RBMs):** A type of neural network that could be trained layer by layer using unsupervised learning. Hinton used RBMs for "pre-training" deep networks before fine-tuning with backpropagation. (Sequel to §II’s Boltzmann Machine — see energy `E(v,h)= -aᵀv -bᵀh - vᵀWh` and the bipartite restriction.)

**How RBMs Work:** A two-layer network where visible units connect to hidden units. Training maximizes the probability of visible units by adjusting weights based on reconstruction error (Contrastive Divergence).

**Limitation:** RBMs were slow to train and didn't scale well — but the *Energy-Based* framing (LeCun et al., 2006) formalized the principle still used in diffusion models: learn an energy landscape.

**Energy-Based Models Unified View (LeCun 2006):** All the 1982-2006 models (Hopfield, Boltzmann, RBM) are **EBMs** — learn `E_θ(x,y)` low for correct pairs, high otherwise. Training = shape the energy surface. This view later justified diffusion (energy = score of noisy data) and JEPA (predictive energy).

**Autoencoders:** Neural networks that learn to compress and reconstruct their inputs. The compressed representation (hidden layer) could then be used for features in other tasks.

**How They Work:** The encoder maps input to a compressed representation; the decoder reconstructs the input from that representation. Training minimizes reconstruction error.

**Application:** Dimensionality reduction, feature learning, denoising.

**The Deep Belief Network (DBN):** Hinton's breakthrough—stacking RBMs to create a deep network that could learn hierarchical features. DBNs were pre-trained layer by layer, then fine-tuned with backpropagation.

**The "Deep Learning" Term:** Coined by Hinton in 2006, it described using neural networks with more than one hidden layer. Many researchers were skeptical—why would deeper networks work when shallow ones had failed?

**ImageNet (2009):** Fei-Fei Li created a massive dataset of 14 million labeled images across 20,000 categories. ImageNet provided the data necessary for deep learning to finally succeed.

**Why ImageNet Mattered:** Previous datasets were too small—neural networks with millions of parameters simply didn't have enough data to learn from. ImageNet changed that.

**GPUs as Game Changers:** Graphics cards (GPUs) designed for video games turned out to be perfect for neural network training. Their massively parallel architecture could process thousands of neurons simultaneously.

**Key Figure:** Andrew Ng was an early advocate for using GPUs for deep learning, demonstrating orders of magnitude speedup over CPUs.

## BOOK VII — JOSHUA: THE DEEP LEARNING BREAKTHROUGH (2012-2015)

### AlexNet and the ImageNet Revolution

**AlexNet (2012):** Alex Krizhevsky, Ilya Sutskever, and Geoffrey Hinton trained a deep convolutional neural network (CNN) on ImageNet and won the annual competition by an enormous margin. This was the watershed moment—deep learning had finally arrived.

**Key Innovations:** 
- **Deep Architecture:** 5 convolutional layers + 3 fully connected layers (60 million parameters).
- **ReLU Activation:** Rectified Linear Units (max(0,x)) that were faster and less prone to vanishing gradients than sigmoid.
- **Dropout:** Randomly dropping neurons during training to prevent overfitting.
- **GPU Training:** Two GPUs for 5-6 days of training—unthinkable without hardware advances.

**Why It Worked:** AlexNet demonstrated that deep neural networks could learn hierarchical features—edges in early layers, shapes in middle layers, objects in deep layers. This hierarchy of abstraction was the key insight.

**The Deep Learning Explosion:** After AlexNet, researchers flooded into deep learning. Convolutional neural networks became the standard for image recognition, achieving superhuman performance on ImageNet.

**Key Contribution:** AlexNet proved that deep learning worked—not just in principle but in practice, on real-world data.

### The Three Missing Pillars of 2012-2015 (Audit Blind Spots #2, #3, #4)

These made “going deep” actually possible — and spawned generative AI:

**1. Dropout (Hinton et al., 2012) — The Regularization Breakthrough:** During AlexNet, Hinton’s team randomly *dropped* 50% of neurons each training step (`p=0.5` in FC layers). It prevents co-adaptation — an ensemble of exponentially many thinned networks. **Effect:** AlexNet’s test error dropped ~2% — without dropout, 60M params overfit. Dropout as a *standalone* paper (Srivastava et al., JMLR 2014) became the most cited regularization trick of the decade, reused in Transformers (0.1 dropout).

**2. Batch Normalization (Ioffe & Szegedy, 2015) — Why Depth Became Trainable:** Normalize each mini-batch to `mean=0, var=1` then scale/shift `γ·x̂ + β`. Solves *internal covariate shift* — previously, deep stacks (20+ layers) exploded/vanished. BN allowed 10× higher learning rates and made 100-layer nets converge. “The reason we could train super-deep networks at all” (Audit). Every ResNet, Transformer, and diffusion U-Net uses BN/LayerNorm descendants.

**3. Residual Networks — ResNet (He et al., 2015) — 100+ Layers Deep:** Kaiming He’s `y = F(x) + x` skip connection. Instead of learning `H(x)`, learn residual `F(x)=H(x)-x`. If identity is optimal, push `F→0` rather than fit identity via nonlinear layers. **Result:** 152-layer ResNet won ImageNet 2015 with 3.57% error (vs human ~5%), *deeper but easier to optimize*. You mention residuals in the Transformer section, but ResNet was the *original* proof (Dec 2015, CVPR 2016). Transformers borrowed it verbatim (`Add & Norm`).

**Infrastructure: TensorFlow (Nov 2015) & PyTorch (Oct 2016) + TPUs (2016):** Before 2015, researchers derived gradients *by hand*. Google’s **TensorFlow** (DistBelief successor, static graph) and Meta’s **PyTorch** (dynamic graphs, `autograd`, Pythonic eager execution) automated differentiation. PyTorch’s `tensor.cuda()` one-liner + dynamic `torch.nn.Module` made debugging intuitive — researchers switched en masse (NeurIPS 2019: ~80% papers used PyTorch). Simultaneously, **Google’s TPUs (2016, ASICs for matmuls)** powered AlphaGo (2016), BERT, and later every Transformer scale-up; Cloud TPUs (2017) + pods let Google train on thousands of chips. **Frameworks + CUDA + TPUs = the loop that made scaling possible.**

**Fast Forward through Time (2013-2014):** The deep learning boom accelerated:
- **Image Classification:** Models outperformed humans on standard benchmarks.
- **Object Detection:** R-CNN and its variants could identify objects in images.
- **Face Recognition:** Deep networks achieved near-human performance.

**Word2Vec (2013):** Tomas Mikolov at Google created Word2Vec, a method for learning word embeddings—dense vector representations of words. Word2Vec showed that words with similar meanings have similar vector representations.

**The Word2Vec Trick:** Train a neural network to predict a word from its neighbors (or vice versa). The hidden layer weights become word embeddings.

**Example:** Vector("king") - Vector("man") + Vector("woman") ≈ Vector("queen"). This demonstrated that Word2Vec captured semantic relationships.

**Why It Mattered:** Word embeddings were the first successful representation of meaning in neural networks—words as points in a continuous vector space, where distance corresponded to semantic similarity.

### The Generative “Missing Middle” — VAEs, GANs, Style Transfer (2013-2015) [Audit #2]

You jump from Word2Vec (2013) to Transformers — skipping the explosion that invented “AI art”:

**Variational Autoencoders — VAEs (Kingma & Welling, arXiv Dec 2013 → ICLR 2014: *Auto-Encoding Variational Bayes*)** and independently Rezende et al. 2014: First *scalable* deep generative models. Encoder `q_φ(z|x)` → Gaussian `N(μ,σ²)`; decoder `p_θ(x|z)`; train via **ELBO + reparameterization trick** `z = μ + σ·ε, ε~N(0,1)` to backprop through sampling. Unlike plain autoencoders, VAEs learn a *continuous, probabilistic* latent space — you can interpolate `z` and generate new data. VQ-VAE-2, β-VAE later fixed blurriness. VAEs became the latent backbone for Stable Diffusion (autoencoder KL).

**Generative Adversarial Networks — GANs (Goodfellow et al., NeurIPS 2014: *Generative Adversarial Nets*) :** *The* dominant generative paradigm before diffusion. Two nets in a minimax game: **Generator G: noise → fake** vs **Discriminator D: real vs fake**. Objective `min_G max_D E[log D(x)] + E[log(1-D(G(z)))]`. DCGAN (Radford 2015) gave architecture rules (strided convs, BatchNorm, no dense layers, 64×64); **WGAN (Arjovsky 2017, Wasserstein distance)** stabilized training; **CycleGAN (2017)** did unpaired image-to-image translation; **StyleGAN / StyleGAN2 / StyleGAN3 (Karras 2018-2021)** invented the “AI face” — high-res, disentangled style control, alias-free. GANs *invented* the AI art boom: DCGAN → “AI portraits” viral 2016, StyleGAN → “This Person Does Not Exist” 2019. Limitation: mode collapse (cover only part of distribution) + instability — why diffusion later won.

**Neural Style Transfer (Gatys et al., arXiv Aug 2015: *A Neural Algorithm of Artistic Style*) :** Showed Gram matrices of VGG features encode *style* vs *content*. Optimize pixels to match content `F^l` + style `G^l`. Made “DeepDream” and “make my photo in Van Gogh style” go viral summer 2015 — first mass-cultural AI generation, before anyone said “generative AI.” Feed-forward variants (Johnson 2016) made it real-time.

Together, VAEs (probabilistic, stable but blurry) + GANs (sharp but unstable) defined the 2014-2019 generative tension that diffusion resolved.

### Diffusion Models — The Physics-Inspired Revolution (2015 → 2020-2022) [Audit #3]

You mention Sora 2 in the timeline but never explain *what* it is:

**Forward diffusion (Sohl-Dickstein et al., ICML 2015):** Slowly add Gaussian noise `x_t = √(1-β_t)·x_{t-1} + √β_t·ε` over T steps (Markov chain) until `x_T ~ N(0,I)`. **Reverse diffusion (learned):** train a U-Net to *denoise* step-by-step, predicting noise `ε_θ(x_t,t)`. Inspired by non-equilibrium thermodynamics (diffusion as physics).

**DDPM — Denoising Diffusion Probabilistic Models (Ho et al., NeurIPS 2020):** The practical breakthrough. Simplified loss `||ε - ε_θ(x_t,t)||²`, showed iterative denoising produces *photorealistic* samples beating GANs on FID, with full mode coverage (no collapse) + stable training. Key follow-ups: **DDIM (2021, faster deterministic sampling)**, **Improved DDPM / ADM (2021)**, **Classifier-Free Guidance (Ho & Salimans 2022)** — the `w` knob that makes text conditioning work.

**Why diffusion dominates image/video now:** **Stable Diffusion (Rombach et al., 2022: latent diffusion — run diffusion in VAE latent space, not pixels, 10× cheaper)** + **DALL-E 2 (2022)** made text→image mainstream. **Sora (OpenAI Feb 2024 preview → Sora 2 Sep 2025)** extends diffusion + Transformer (DiT) to *video* — same denoising idea but over space-time patches. The theoretical shift: *don’t* sample in one shot (GAN/VAE); *iteratively refine noise* — like sculpting from static, 50-250 steps. Quality vs speed tradeoff (NFE) is why you see `NFE ↓` tables: GAN 1 step vs diffusion 250 steps — but diffusion wins on diversity.

**Recurrent Neural Networks (RNNs):** Neural networks designed for sequences—they maintain a hidden state that's passed from one time step to the next, allowing them to process variable-length sequences.

**How They Work:** At each time step, the RNN takes the current input and the previous hidden state, producing the current output and updated hidden state.

**Key Limitation:** Vanishing gradients—as sequences get longer, gradients shrink, preventing learning of long-range dependencies.

**LSTM Networks (1997):** Sepp Hochreiter and Jürgen Schmidhuber invented Long Short-Term Memory networks, which use gated cells to remember information for long periods. LSTMs became the dominant architecture for sequence modeling.

**How LSTMs Work:** Input gate, forget gate, and output gate control what information is stored, discarded, and output. The cell state acts as a "conveyor belt" that carries information across long sequences.

**GRU (2014):** Cho et al. introduced Gated Recurrent Units, a simpler variant of LSTM with only two gates. GRUs became popular for their efficiency.

**Sequence-to-Sequence Models (2014):** Sutskever, Vinyals, and Le introduced Seq2Seq models for machine translation. An encoder RNN processes the input sequence into a fixed-length vector, and a decoder RNN generates output from that vector.

**Key Concept:** The encoder compresses the entire input into a single vector—the "context vector"—which the decoder uses to generate output.

**Limitation:** The fixed-length context vector becomes a bottleneck for long sequences—important information gets "squeezed" into a limited vector space.

### The Missing RL Prequel — DQN & AlphaGo (2013-2017) [Audit #1]

You have the RL *reasoning* era (Kimi K1.5, DeepSeek-R1, 2024-25), but you skipped the RL *breakthroughs* that funded DeepMind and proved RL could be general:

**DQN — Deep Q-Networks (Mnih et al., DeepMind, arXiv Dec 2013 → Nature Feb 2015: *Human-level control through deep reinforcement learning*):** First end-to-end deep RL from *raw pixels*. Architecture: CNN (3 conv layers) → fully connected → Q-values for each Atari action, trained with **experience replay** (1M frame buffer, break correlation) + **target network** (frozen copy updated every C steps, stabilize bootstrap). Input: 84×84×4 stacked frames. Output: `Q(s,a;θ)`. Loss: `r + γ max_a' Q(s',a';θ⁻) - Q(s,a;θ)`. Same network + hyperparameters achieved **human-level on 29/49 Atari games** (e.g., Breakout discovered tunneling strategy). No handcrafted features — proof CNN + Q-learning could learn control *from vision*.

**AlphaGo (Silver et al., DeepMind, Nature Mar 2016):** The *Sputnik moment* before Transformers. Beat **Lee Sedol 4-1** (Seoul, Mar 2016) — a 9-dan Go legend in a game with `10^170` positions, deemed a decade away. System: **Supervised policy network** (predict human moves, 57% accuracy) → **RL policy network** (self-play, win 80% vs supervised) → **Value network** (predict winner from position) + **MCTS** (Monte Carlo Tree Search) that guides search with both networks. Elo ~3,140 (above any human). AlphaGo Fan (Oct 2015) beat European champion Fan Hui 5-0 first.

**AlphaGo Zero (Oct 2017) → AlphaZero (Science Dec 2017-2018):** Removed human data entirely. **Zero human knowledge, tabula rasa self-play** from random weights, using *only* the rules. Single ResNet `f_θ(s) → (p, v)` (policy + value) + MCTS, with `~4.9M` self-play games. Result: **AlphaGo Zero beat AlphaGo 100-0 after 3 days** (4.9M steps), then **AlphaZero (same algorithm, no Go-specific tweaks) mastered Chess (beat Stockfish 28 wins, 72 draws, 0 losses with 4 TPUs, Dec 2017), Shogi, and Go within *hours*** — proving RL *alone* could discover superhuman, creative strategies (AlphaZero’s “immortal” piece sacrifices). Also introduced **PPO (Schulman 2017, OpenAI)** — the on-policy RL that later powers RLHF/InstructGPT — and foreshadowed **MuZero (2020, learns its own model, no rules given)**.

**Why this funds everything after:** DeepMind’s $400M/acquisition → Google TPUs → the RLHF/PPO pipeline that later aligns ChatGPT/Claude/Kimi-K2. Without DQN→AlphaGo, RL stays a toy gridworld.

## BOOK VIII — DANIEL: THE EYES OF AI — COMPUTER VISION (1959-2026)

Vision was AI's first and hardest sense. The rest of this document treats "perception" as a thing the Transformer absorbed (§IX, §X); this section is the missing arc — the 65 years of research that taught machines to see, from single neurons in a cat's cortex to "Segment Anything." Computer vision is where deep learning won its first war (AlexNet, §VII) and where the field's deepest open problem still lives: **understanding** rather than *recognizing*.

### The Biological Seed — How the Brain Sees (1959-1998)

**Hubel & Wiesel (1959-1962):** Torsten Wiesel and David Hubel recorded from cat and monkey visual cortex and discovered **orientation-selective cells** — neurons that fire only for edges at a specific angle, organized in columns. Simple cells → complex cells → hypercomplex cells: a **hierarchy of feature detectors**. Nobel Prize in Medicine 1981. This is the single most important paper in vision AI history — every CNN is a direct descendant of this experimental fact.

**Neocognitron (Fukushima, 1980):** Kunihiko Fukushima's *Neocognitron* (Biological Cybernetics) was the **first convolutional neural network** — alternating layers of simple (feature-detecting) and complex (shift-invariant) cells, trained by unsupervised self-organization, achieving shift-invariant pattern recognition. It was *directly* inspired by Hubel & Wiesel. **Every CNN from LeNet to Kimi K3's MoonViT traces its lineage here.**

**LeNet-5 (LeCun, 1989 → 1998):** Yann LeCun built the first backprop-trained convolutional network for **handwritten digit recognition** (USPS, 1989), then the canonical **LeNet-5** (1998) with 7 layers — 2 conv + 2 subsample + 2 FC + Gaussian — trained with backpropagation on **MNIST** (70k digits, 10 classes), which became the "hello world" of deep learning. Key insight: **local receptive fields + weight sharing** = translation invariance with far fewer parameters than a fully-connected net. Commercialized by AT&T for check reading — the first deep learning product.

**Key Concept:** The CNN inductive bias — *locality* (each neuron sees a patch), *translation equivariance* (weight sharing), and *hierarchical composition* (edges → parts → objects) — is the biological-visual hypothesis, written into silicon.

### The Classical Era — Features by Hand (1999-2012)

Before deep learning, "features" were *engineered* by hand. This was a mature, pragmatic engineering field:

- **SIFT (Lowe, 1999 → 2004):** Scale-Invariant Feature Transform — detected keypoints robust to scale, rotation, and lighting change, described by 128-d histograms of gradient orientation. For a decade, SIFT was *the* feature for matching, stitching (panoramas), 3D reconstruction, and object recognition.
- **Viola-Jones (2001):** Paul Viola & Michael Jones — the **first real-time face detector**: Haar-like features + AdaBoost + a **cascade** that rejects non-faces cheaply. Ran at ~15 fps on 2001 CPUs; shipped in every digital camera. Pure engineered vision at its peak.
- **HOG (Dalal & Triggs, 2005):** Histogram of Oriented Gradients for pedestrian detection — normalized gradient histograms over dense grids. SIFT's sibling; the basis of the classic person detector.
- **DPM (Felzenszwalb et al., 2008-2010):** Deformable Part Models — "parts + spring" object models, the best pre-deep detector; won Pascal VOC multiple years.
- **Pascal VOC (2005-2012):** The benchmark that codified "detection/segmentation/classification" — 20 object classes, precision-recall metrics. **ImageNet (Deng et al., 2009; Fei-Fei Li)** then scaled that idea to 14M images / 20k categories (§VI). The 2012 ImageNet competition is where deep learning detonated (§VII).

**Why It Mattered:** SIFT/Viola-Jones/HOG/DPM proved *features + classifier* could do real perception — and simultaneously proved hand-engineered features were the bottleneck. The 2012 watershed happened precisely because **learned** features replaced them.

### The Deep Watershed (2012-2015)

**AlexNet → ZFNet → VGG → GoogLeNet/Inception → ResNet:** the full story is in §VII (ReLU, Dropout, BatchNorm, ResNet — the "missing pillars"). The vision-specific takeaway: from 2012 to 2015, ImageNet top-5 error fell from **15.3% (AlexNet) → 11.2% (ZFNet) → 7.3% (VGG-16, all 3×3 convs) → 6.7% (GoogLeNet, 1×1 "bottleneck" convs) → 3.57% (ResNet-152, 2015, *below human* ~5%)**. In four years, deep CNNs went from surprising to superhuman, and "convolution" became the default layer of vision.

### Object Detection — The R-CNN → YOLO Revolution (2014-2020)

Detection (find *and* box every object) is where vision engineering got really hard — and where the 2014-2017 era produced the pattern used by every detector since:

- **R-CNN (Girshick et al., CVPR 2014):** Region-based CNN — run selective-search to propose ~2,000 candidate boxes, run a CNN on each, classify + regress. Slow (~50s/image) but a massive jump — the template for "propose → classify."
- **Fast R-CNN (Girshick, ICCV 2015)** — RoI pooling over one shared feature map; **Faster R-CNN (Ren et al., NeurIPS 2015)** — the **Region Proposal Network (RPN)**: the *network itself* proposes boxes, making detection end-to-end trainable and near-real-time. Faster R-CNN's anchor-based two-stage design dominated 2016-2020.
- **YOLO (Redmon et al., CVPR 2016):** *"You Only Look Once"* — the first **one-stage detector**: divide the image into a grid, predict boxes + class scores in one forward pass. 45 fps (fast YOLO: 155 fps). Joseph Redmon's YOLO family (YOLO9000 2017, YOLOv3 2018 — 53-layer Darknet) spawned an entire open ecosystem still alive in 2026 (YOLOv8/9/10/11, Ultralytics). **Two-stage (accurate, slower) vs one-stage (fast) became the field's permanent tension.**
- **SSD (Liu et al., ECCV 2016)** — one-stage + multi-scale feature maps; **RetinaNet + Focal Loss (Lin et al., ICCV 2017)** — the *focal loss* `FL(p_t) = -(1-p_t)^γ log(p_t)` down-weights easy negatives, letting one-stage detectors finally match two-stage accuracy; **DETR (Carion et al., ECCV 2020)** — replaced anchors with a **Transformer** + bipartite matching (a direct §IX premonition); Grounding DINO (2023) made detection *language-promptable*.

### Segmentation — From Pixels to Masks (2015-2023)

- **FCN (Long et al., CVPR 2015):** Fully Convolutional Networks — replace FC layers with 1×1 convs and **upsample** (deconv/transposed conv) to pixel-level predictions; first end-to-end semantic segmentation.
- **U-Net (Ronneberger et al., MICCAI 2015):** Encoder-decoder with **skip connections** — the U shape. Dominated biomedical segmentation (cells, tumors, organs); its skip-connection design is also a direct ancestor of Transformer `Add & Norm` residual thinking. U-Nets later became the *backbone of diffusion models* (§VII) — Stable Diffusion's denoiser is a U-Net.
- **SegNet (2015), PSPNet (2017, pyramid pooling), DeepLab family (2016-2018, atrous convs + CRF):** scaled segmentation to scene parsing.
- **Mask R-CNN (He et al., ICCV 2017 best paper):** Faster R-CNN + a mask branch — **instance segmentation** (each object gets its own mask). The workhorse of everything from autonomous driving labels to video editing.
- **SAM — Segment Anything (Kirillov et al., Meta, Apr 2023):** The "GPT of segmentation." A **promptable** model (click, box, or text → mask) trained on **SA-1B** — the largest segmentation dataset ever built (11M images, **1.1B masks**, generated by the model itself in a data flywheel). Zero-shot segmentation of anything, no task-specific fine-tuning. MedSAM, SAM2 (video, 2024) followed. **SAM is the clearest statement that vision had entered its foundation-model era.**

### Self-Supervised Vision — Learning Without Labels (2019-2023)

ImageNet labels are expensive; the 2019-2023 revolution learned visual features from *data structure alone*:

- **MoCo (He et al., 2019), SimCLR (Chen et al., ICML 2020):** **Contrastive learning** — pull augmented views of the same image together, push others apart (InfoNCE loss, §IX). SimCLR showed a 4× bigger ResNet trained contrastively matches supervised ImageNet accuracy.
- **BYOL (Grill et al., NeurIPS 2020):** contrastive *without negatives* — a teacher-student pair with EMA; surprising and hugely influential.
- **DINO (Caron et al., ICCV 2021):** Self-distillation with **no labels** — and its attention maps turned out to be *semantic*: DINO's features spontaneously segment objects (the "self-attention maps" visualization everyone has seen). DINOv2 (2023) distilled the whole paradigm into a single strong backbone.
- **MAE — Masked Autoencoders (He et al., CVPR 2022):** The BERT trick for images — mask 75% of patches, reconstruct them. *"An Image is Worth 16x16 Words"* (§X) made masking natural. MAE showed a plain ViT trained with masking beats supervised pretraining.

**Key Insight:** Self-supervision broke vision's data bottleneck — you no longer needed labels, just pixels. CLIP (§X) then broke the *modality* bottleneck by aligning pixels to language.

### Vision Transformers (2021-) and 3D, Motion & Geometry (2015-2024)

**ViT (Dosovitskiy et al., ICLR 2021) — patches beat pixels:** the full story is in §X. The fallout: **Swin (ICLR 2021 best paper, hierarchical windows), DEiT (data-efficient training), non-local networks (Wang et al. 2018, attention-in-CNN prequel), and ConvNeXt (2022, "a ConvNet for the 2020s")** — the 2021-2023 "ViT vs CNN" war that ended in a draw and a merger (hybrids like MoonViT-V2 in Kimi K3, §XII).

**3D & motion:** **Structure-from-Motion** (SfM) + multi-view stereo built sparse 3D from images for decades; deep learning added: **optical flow — FlowNet (2015), RAFT (ECCV 2020 best paper)**; **pose estimation — OpenPose (Cao et al., CVPR 2017, real-time multi-person 2D pose)**; **NeRF — Neural Radiance Fields (Mildenhall et al., ECCV 2020):** represent a scene as a continuous 5D function `(x,y,z,θ,φ) → (RGB,σ)` trained by volumetric ray marching — photorealistic novel views from a handful of photos, instantly the standard for 3D capture; **3D Gaussian Splatting (Kerbl et al., SIGGRAPH 2023):** NeRF's successor — explicit 3D Gaussians rendered by splatting, real-time (100+ fps) photoreal novel views; now the backbone of photogrammetry, VR, and smartphone 3D scanning.

### Generative Vision, OCR, Medical & Foundation Models (2014-2026)

- **Generative vision** (full depth in §VII): DCGAN (2015) → Pix2Pix (2017, paired translation) → CycleGAN (2017, unpaired) → StyleGAN (2018, "This Person Does Not Exist") → **diffusion (2020-2022, Stable Diffusion/DALL-E/Sora)** → ControlNet (2023, pose/edge-conditioned generation).
- **Faces & biometrics:** DeepFace (Facebook, 2014, 97.35% LFW), FaceNet (Schroff et al., CVPR 2015 — **triplet loss** `max(0, d(a,p) - d(a,n) + α)`, 128-d embeddings), ArcFace (Deng et al., 2018, additive angular margin) — the stack behind every phone Face ID and surveillance system.
- **OCR & documents:** CRNN (2015) end-to-end text recognition; **TrOCR (2021)** Transformer OCR; by 2024-2026 document AI (Nougat, Florence-2, Qwen2.5-VL) reads *layout-aware* PDFs — the "OCR" of the LLM era.
- **Medical imaging:** CheXNet (Rajpurkar et al., Stanford, 2017) — chest X-ray pneumonia detection exceeding radiologist average; followed by FDA-cleared radiology AI, pathology foundation models (Virchow, 2023), **MedSAM** (SAM for medical images), and retina/cardiology screening at scale.
- **Foundation models & the perception ceiling:** Florence (Meta, 2023), **Florence-2** (Microsoft, 2024 — one prompt-driven model for caption/detection/segmentation/OCR/grounding, 0.2-0.8B), Grounding DINO + SAM (ground-and-segment), DINOv2 backbones — vision's "foundation layer" is now generic, frozen, and shared across every downstream task, exactly like BERT/GPT in NLP. **What vision still can't do** is what children do effortlessly: count, reason about occlusion and physics, and *understand* a scene — which is why multimodal LLMs (LLaVA, GPT-4V, Gemini, K3 MoonViT, §XVIII) and world models (JEPA, §XI) exist.

**Key Figures:** **Kunihiko Fukushima** (Neocognitron), **Yann LeCun** (LeNet, CNN doctrine), **Fei-Fei Li** (ImageNet), **Alex Krizhevsky** (AlexNet), **Ross Girshick** (R-CNN/Fast/Faster, "Mr. Detection"), **Joseph Redmon** (YOLO), **Kaiming He** (ResNet, Faster R-CNN, Mask R-CNN, MAE — arguably the most prolific vision researcher alive), **Alexander Kirillov** (SAM), **Ben Mildenhall** (NeRF).

## BOOK IX — ISAIAH: THE ATTENTION REVOLUTION (2015-2017)

### From Bottlenecks to Attention

**The Attention Mechanism (2015):** Bahdanau, Cho, and Bengio introduced attention in sequence-to-sequence models. Instead of compressing everything into a single vector, attention allows the decoder to look back at the entire input sequence, focusing on relevant parts.

**How Attention Works:** At each decoding step, the model computes attention weights over the input sequence—the "importance" of each input word for generating the current output word. The decoder then uses a weighted sum of input representations.

**The Intuition:** When translating "I am walking the dog" to French, at the step of generating "chien" (dog), the model should "pay attention" to "dog" in the input, not "walking."

**Why Attention Was Revolutionary:** 
- Solved the bottleneck problem—the model could refer directly to the input.
- Interpretable—you could see which words the model was focusing on.
- Improved performance dramatically—attention became standard in Seq2Seq.

**Self-Attention / Intra-Attention (2016):** Researchers realized that attention could be applied within a single sequence—each word paying attention to other words in the same sequence. This was the predecessor to Transformers.

**How Self-Attention Works:** Each word computes attention over all words in the sequence, learning dependencies between different positions.

**Why It Matters:** Self-attention captures long-range dependencies—a word can attend to any other word, regardless of distance.

**Modern Hopfield ⇔ Attention (Ramsauer et al., 2020-2021: *Hopfield Networks is All You Need*) — Closing the Loop:** Formal proof that Transformer attention *is* a Hopfield update with exponential storage (`β=1/√d`). This retroactively justifies §II: 1982 energy minimization *is* 2017 attention. See also §VI EBMs.

### The Training Stack Under the Transformer — Losses, Optimizers, Learning Rates & Numerical Stability [Deep Dive]

Attention gives the Transformer its *capacity*; this is the layer that made it *trainable*. The 2017 paper's elegance hid a brutal engineering reality — the model only worked because of a specific stack of gradient machinery built up over 70 years. Every later model in this document (BERT, GPT, Claude, Kimi K2/K3, even SupraLabs' 100M models in §XIII) runs some version of this stack.

**1. Loss Functions — what the model is actually minimizing.** A Transformer is trained by **maximum likelihood / cross-entropy**: for each token, minimize `L = -Σ log p_θ(x_t | x_<t)` — equivalently maximize the probability of the observed next token. Because decoding is done token-by-token with teacher forcing (the true previous tokens are fed during training; Williams & Zipser 1989; scheduled sampling softens this, Bengio et al. 2015), the loss decomposes over positions. Key relatives used across this document:
- **KL divergence** (Kullback & Leibler, 1951) — measures distance between distributions; used for distillation (Hinton 2015), RLHF KL penalties (§X), and diffusion losses.
- **InfoNCE / contrastive loss** (van den Oord et al., 2018) — `L = -log[exp(sim(q,k+)/τ) / Σ exp(sim(q,k_i)/τ)]`; the engine of CLIP (§X), self-supervised vision (SimCLR, DINO, §VIII), and embeddings.
- **Triplet loss** (FaceNet, §VIII) and **focal loss** (`FL(p_t)=-(1-p_t)^γ log p_t`, RetinaNet 2017, §VIII) for vision; **Dice loss** for segmentation.
- **Adversarial loss** (GANs, §VII) — the minimax game; **diffusion loss** (DDPM, §VII) — denoising MSE on predicted noise.
- **Preference losses** (DPO/KTO, §X) — train from human choices directly, no reward model.
- **z-loss** (from ST-MoE, Zoph et al. 2022) — `L_z = α·log Σ exp(logits)` prevents router/output logits from drifting; used by both Kimi K3 (2.78T) and Supra2-100M.
- **Multi-Token Prediction / MTP** (Gloeckle et al., Meta 2024; adopted by Kimi K2/K3): predict the *next k tokens* per position — improves sample efficiency and adds a "planning" signal (§XI).

**2. Optimizers — the descent machinery.** All neural training is **stochastic gradient descent** on the loss landscape, refined over 70 years:
- **SGD** (Robbins & Monro, 1951 — "A Stochastic Approximation Method") — update `θ ← θ - η∇L` on random minibatches. **Momentum** (Polyak, 1964) — accumulate velocity `v ← βv - η∇L; θ ← θ + v` to cross ravines. **Nesterov momentum** (1983) — look ahead before stepping.
- **Adaptive per-parameter learning rates:** **AdaGrad** (Duchi et al., 2011) — divide by accumulated squared gradients (works for sparse features, decays too fast); **RMSProp** (Hinton, 2012 lecture) — use a moving average instead; **Adam** (Kingma & Ba, ICLR 2015) — combines momentum + RMSProp with bias correction:
  `m_t = β1 m_{t-1} + (1-β1) g_t`, `v_t = β2 v_{t-1} + (1-β2) g_t²`, `θ ← θ - η·m̂_t/(√v̂_t + ε)`.
  Adam (with β1=0.9, β2=0.95-0.999) became *the* optimizer of the Transformer era — "AdamW + cosine + warmup" is the default recipe of GPT-2/BERT through LLaMA-2.
- **AdamW** (Loshchilov & Hutter, 2017/ICLR 2019) — *decoupled weight decay*: subtract λθ from the weights directly instead of folding L2 into the gradient. Small change, big stability win; the standard since 2019.
- **The scale-optimizers:** **LARS** (2017) and **LAMB** (2019) — layer-wise adaptive LR for huge batches (BERT pretraining at 64k batch); **Adafactor** (Shazeer & Stern, 2018) — memory-efficient Adam for T5; **Shampoo** (Gupta et al., 2018-2021) — Kronecker-factored second-order; **Lion** (Chen et al., 2023, discovered by *symbolic program search*) — sign-based, ~2× fewer FLOPs than Adam; **Sophia** (2023) — second-order curvature, ~2× faster wall-clock.
- **Muon** (Jordan et al., 2024) — the 2025-2026 outlier: treat each weight *matrix* as a matrix, run SGD-with-momentum, then **orthogonalize the update with a Newton-Schulz iteration** (singular values pushed toward 1). "<1% FLOP overhead," ~2× faster convergence on LLMs; adopted by Kimi K2/K3 (Per-Head Muon, §XII) and by SupraLabs' SupraBrain-50M. A full account of why "Muon is the first real advance since Adam" is still being written, but it is already the defining optimizer of the 2T-era (§XXIII).
- **Gradient clipping** (Pascanu et al., 2013) — cap `‖g‖` to avoid loss spikes; **weight decay** for regularization; **EMA of weights** for smoother inference (standard in diffusion & RL).

**3. Learning Rates & Schedules — the shape of the descent.** The learning rate η is the single most sensitive hyperparameter:
- **Warmup** — ramp η from 0 to peak over the first 1-2% of steps; Vaswani's original Transformer used the schedule `lr = d_model^(-0.5) · min(step^(-0.5), step·warmup_steps^(-1.5))` with 4,000 warmup steps. Goyal et al. (2017) formalized the **linear scaling rule** for large batches.
- **Cosine decay** (Loshchilov & Hutter, 2016, SGDR) — anneal η along a half-cosine to ~0; the 2018-2023 default (GPT-3, LLaMA-1/2, Mistral). **OneCycle** (Smith & Topin, 2018) — warmup then anneal within each cycle.
- **WSD — Warmup-Stable-Decay** (MiniCPM, Hu et al., 2024): warmup → hold peak for 60-80% of training → rapidly decay the last 10-25%. Why it won: *you don't commit to a token budget up front* — train at peak as long as you like, **branch checkpoints** at any point and decay each branch for different downstream uses (the trick behind Llama 3's 8B/70B/405B from one run). DeepSeek-V2, Gemma 2, and Supra2-100M all use WSD-family schedules.
- **LR for fine-tuning** is 10-100× smaller than pretraining (2e-5 vs 1e-3-6e-4); **RLHF** uses tiny LRs (1e-6-1e-5) because the model is already good (§X).

**4. Numerical stability & precision — making the gradients survive.**
- **Normalization:** **LayerNorm** (Ba et al., 2016) — normalize over features per token (vs BatchNorm over the batch, Ioffe 2015, §VII); **RMSNorm** (Zhang & Sennrich, 2019) — drop the mean (cheaper); **Pre-LN** (2020) — normalize before sublayers, the modern default; **QK-Norm** (RMSNorm on Q and K) — prevents attention logit blowup at high LR; the reason Supra2 and Qwen3 train stably at 1e-3.
- **Mixed precision:** FP16/BF16 training (Micikevicius et al., 2017; NVIDIA) with **loss scaling**; **BF16** (2019) gave the same exponent range as FP32, killing most overflow; **FP8** (H100, 2023) for training; **MXFP4/MXFP8** (2024-2026, K3 §XII). Every 2026 model trains in bf16/fp8 and the *stability tricks* (z-loss, QK-Norm, WSD) exist to keep that precision from destroying the loss landscape.
- **Memory engineering:** gradient **accumulation** (fake large batches); **activation checkpointing** (Chen et al., 2016 — recompute, don't store); **ZeRO** (Rajbhandari et al., 2020) and **FSDP** (PyTorch, 2021) — shard optimizer state/gradients/weights across devices; **DeepSpeed** (Microsoft, 2020) packaging it all. This is what let 175B → 2.78T parameters ever fit in a cluster.

**The throughline:** the Transformer's 2017 paper introduced *architecture*; everything that made it train at scale — AdamW, warmup+cosine/WSD, LayerNorm/RMSNorm/QK-Norm, mixed precision, ZeRO/FSDP, and now Muon — is the **training stack**. When §XIII's Supra2-100M "runs the same stability tricks as K3 at 2.78T," this is the stack it means. When DeepSeek-R1 and Kimi K1.5 (RL, §XI) needed stable *rollout* training, they leaned on the same gradient machinery — with RL-specific losses (GRPO, PPO) described in §X.

**The Transformer Architecture (2017):** Vaswani et al. introduced the "Attention Is All You Need" paper, proposing an architecture based entirely on attention, with no recurrence or convolution.

**The Transformer's Core Ideas:**

1. **Scaled Dot-Product Attention:**
   - Query (Q), Key (K), Value (V) matrices.
   - Attention(Q,K,V) = softmax(QKᵀ/√d)V
   - The scaling factor prevents dot products from growing too large.

2. **Multi-Head Attention:**
   - Multiple attention heads learn different attention patterns.
   - Heads are concatenated and linearly transformed.
   - Enables the model to attend to different relationships simultaneously.

3. **Positional Encoding:**
   - Since the Transformer processes all tokens at once, it needs positional information.
   - Sinusoidal encodings: PE(pos,2i) = sin(pos/10000²ⁱ/ᵈ), PE(pos,2i+1) = cos(pos/10000²ⁱ/ᵈ).
   - These encodings are added to token embeddings.

4. **Feed-Forward Networks:**
   - Pointwise (per-position) fully connected layers.
   - Usually two layers with a hidden dimension 4× the input dimension.

5. **Residual Connections and Layer Normalization:**
   - Residual connections: output = F(input) + input
   - Layer normalization stabilizes training.

6. **Encoder-Decoder Structure:**
   - Encoder: stack of 6 layers, each with MHA and FFN.
   - Decoder: stack of 6 layers, with masked MHA, cross-attention, and FFN.

**Why Transformers Were Revolutionary:**

- **Parallelization:** Unlike RNNs (sequential), Transformers process all tokens simultaneously, enabling much faster training on GPUs.
- **Long-Range Dependencies:** RNNs and LSTMs struggled with long sequences; Transformers handle them naturally.
- **Scalability:** Transformers scale well—you can just add more layers, bigger hidden dimensions, and more data.
- **Flexibility:** The same architecture works for language, vision, audio—any sequential data.

**The Transformer's Key Insight:** Attention is sufficient. You don't need recurrence, convolution, or any other mechanism. Just attention, feed-forward networks, and residual connections.

**BERT (2018):** Google's breakthrough model that used the Transformer encoder for language understanding. BERT was pre-trained on massive text corpora using two objectives:
1. **Masked Language Modeling:** Predict masked words in text.
2. **Next Sentence Prediction:** Predict if two sentences are sequential.

**Why BERT Was Important:** BERT achieved state-of-the-art on 11 NLP tasks. It showed that pre-training + fine-tuning was the path forward—you could train one giant model and adapt it to many tasks.

**GPT-1 (2018):** OpenAI's first Transformer-based language model. GPT-1 used the decoder half of the Transformer and was pre-trained on books to predict the next word.

**Key Approach:** Generative pre-training—language modeling on unlabeled data, followed by supervised fine-tuning on specific tasks.

**GPT-2 (2019):** A much larger model (1.5B parameters) that demonstrated "zero-shot" capabilities—it could perform tasks like translation and summarization without explicit fine-tuning.

**Key Insight:** Language models learn a surprising amount of knowledge about the world from text—concepts like translation, question answering, and even reasoning emerge from next-word prediction.

**Why GPT-2 Was Controversial:** OpenAI initially refused to release it, citing concerns about misuse (generating fake news, misinformation). This highlighted the growing tension between progress and safety.

## BOOK X — THE KINGS: THE SCALING ERA (2020-2023)

### Bigger Models, Better Performance

**GPT-3 (2020):** 175 billion parameters—10× larger than any previous non-sparse model. GPT-3 demonstrated:
1. **Few-Shot Learning:** With a few examples in the context, GPT-3 could learn new tasks without weight updates.
2. **Smooth Scaling:** Performance improved predictably with model size.
3. **Emergent Abilities:** Arithmetic, reasoning, and code generation emerged at scale.

**Key Concept:** In-context learning—the model learns from examples within the context window, without gradient updates. This is fundamentally different from fine-tuning.

**The Context Window:** GPT-3 has a 2048-token context window, limiting how many examples can be given in-context.

**DeepSeek LLM (2023):** Refined scaling laws with 67B and 7B models. Key findings:
1. **Data Quality Matters:** Higher quality data favors scaling model size over data volume.
2. **Model Scale Representation:** Non-embedding FLOPs/token (M) is more accurate than parameter count.
3. **Hyperparameter Scaling:** Batch size and learning rate scale predictably with compute.

**Scaling Law Formula:** 
- M_opt ∝ C^0.524 (model scaling)
- D_opt ∝ C^0.476 (data scaling)
- Where C is compute budget.

**Key Insight:** The relationship between model size, data size, and compute can be precisely modeled, enabling performance prediction at larger scales.

**LLaMA (2023):** Meta's open-source models that democratized AI. LLaMA 2 (70B) showed that carefully curated data could achieve GPT-3's performance with fewer parameters.

**Key Contribution:** LLaMA made frontier-level AI accessible to researchers without massive compute budgets — and directly spawned the *desktop-local* movement (see §XIV).

### The Vision-Language Bridge — ViT & CLIP (2021) [Audit #4]

You mention K3’s “native vision” but skipped the shift that made *any* multimodal model possible:

**ViT — Vision Transformer (Dosovitskiy et al., ICLR 2021: *An Image Is Worth 16x16 Words*):** Proved a *pure Transformer* (no convolutions) beats CNNs: split image into 16×16 patches, linear embed → Transformer encoder + learned positional embeddings, pre-train on JFT-300M then fine-tune. ViT-L/16 outperformed BiT-ResNet on ImageNet with fewer FLOPs. ViT → **Swin, DeiT, MAE**, and later **MoonViT-V2 in Kimi K3** (which you note is “trained from scratch, shared image/video, pixel-shuffle 4×”). Without ViT, vision stays CNN.

**CLIP (Radford et al., ICML 2021 + OpenAI blog Jan 2021: *Learning Transferable Visual Models From Natural Language Supervision*):** Contrastive learning on **400M image-text pairs** scraped from the web: image encoder (ViT-B/32 or ResNet-50) + text encoder (Transformer) trained to *maximize* cosine similarity for true pairs, minimize for negatives.

*→ The full multimodal story — captioning, VQA, Flamingo/BLIP-2/LLaVA, GPT-4V/Gemini/GPT-4o, ImageBind, audio/video LLMs — is §XVIII, The Senses of AI.* Result: **zero-shot** ImageNet 76.2% (no supervised labels), and *transferable* representation: prompt `"a photo of a {class}"` → classify anything. **CLIP is the *bridge***: It’s why **DALL-E (2021), Stable Diffusion (2022, uses CLIP text encoder), GPT-4V (2023), Flamingo, LLaVA, and Kimi K3’s MoonViT** can connect words ↔ pixels. Elon’s joke: “CLIP is the API between language and vision.” Without CLIP, multimodal AI doesn’t exist.

### The Efficiency Breakthroughs — FlashAttention, Mamba & Ring Attention (2022-2023) [Audit #2]

You mention KDA/MLA (linear attention) but missed their *predecessors* that made 1M-context even possible in a quadratic world:

**FlashAttention (Dao et al., NeurIPS 2022: *FlashAttention*, v2 Dec 2023):** Standard attention is **HBM-bound**, not compute-bound: O(N²) reads/writes of the N×N matrix between slow GPU HBM and fast SRAM kill you. FlashAttention is **IO-aware tiling**: split Q,K,V into blocks that *fit in SRAM*, compute partial softmax online with `m = max, l = sumexp` statistics, never materialize full matrix. Result: **exact** attention, **O(N²·d) FLOPs unchanged but O(N·d) HBM traffic** (optimal), **2-4× wall-clock speedup + O(N) memory** (not O(N²)). **Every model since GPT-4/Claude 3/Kimi K2/K3 uses it** (PyTorch 2.0 `scaled_dot_product_attention` = Flash). v2 (2023) added parallelism across sequence dim → 2× over v1, ~225 TFLOPs/s on A100 (72% util); v3 (2024) Hopper TMA/WGMMA → 740 TFLOPs BF16, 1.2 PFLOPs FP8.

**Mamba (Gu & Dao, arXiv Dec 2023: *Mamba: Linear-Time Sequence Modeling with Selective SSMs*):** Challenges Transformer quadratically: a **State-Space Model** (SSM, from control theory: `h' = Ah + Bx, y = Ch`) with **selective** gating (A,B,C depend on input) → **linear O(N) scaling**, constant memory recurrent state. On language, Mamba matches Transformer at 3B but scales *linearly* to 1M tokens without FlashAttention. You *support* Mamba in Transformers.js v4 (via `com.microsoft.QMoE`-adjacent ops) but never explained *why*: it’s the serious sub-quadratic challenger before **KDA (your 3×KDA + 1 MLA block in K3)** won by hybridizing linear recurrence + sparse global attention.

**Ring Attention (Liu et al., arXiv Oct 2023: *Ring Attention with Blockwise Transformers*) + context-parallelism cousins:** How you *train* 1M: shard sequence across devices in a ring, each device computes blockwise attention `softmax(Q_block · K_ring)` while overlapping KV communication (DistFlashAttn variant). No single device holds N×N. Without Ring/Striped/DistFlashAttn + **context parallelism** (your §XII’s `Context Parallelism: splitting sequence across devices`), 1M prefill OOMs even with Flash.

Together: Flash makes quadratic *fast*, Mamba makes it *linear*, Ring makes it *distributed*.

### The Democratization & PEFT Layer — LoRA, Quantization, QLoRA (2021-2023) [Audit #5]

You mention “Quantization-Aware Training” in K3, but missed the grassroots tools that let *anyone* fine-tune a 7B on one GPU — why `q4` *works*:

**LoRA — Low-Rank Adaptation (Hu et al., arXiv Oct 2021 → ICLR 2022):** Don’t fine-tune `W₀ (d×k, 7B params)`. Freeze it, inject tiny adapter `ΔW = B·A` where `B(d×r), A(r×k), r = 8-64 << d` (e.g., 0.1% params). Forward `h = W₀x + BAx` (scaled by α/r). Train only BA. Result: fine-tune 7B on *one* 24GB GPU, swap adapters per task (`adapter_model.bin` ~10MB), merge at inference. Standard for every open-model fine-tune since 2022.

**GPTQ (Frantar et al., arXiv Oct 2022: *GPTQ: Accurate Post-Training Quantization*) & AWQ (Lin et al., arXiv Jun 2023: *AWQ: Activation-aware Weight Quantization*):** Why `q4` doesn’t tank performance. **GPTQ**: layer-wise second-order quantization (Hessian `H = 2XXᵀ`) + greedy column-wise rounding, accurate to 3-4 bits. **AWQ**: observes activation outliers → protect 1% salient channels (scale them up before quantization). Both achieve **4-bit (q4, Q4_K_M in GGUF) ≈ lossless** (perplexity +0.1) — enabling 7B in ~4GB, 70B in ~40GB. Your `dtype: 'q4'/'q4f16'/'q1'` in Transformers.js v3/v4 leans on these advances upstream.

**QLoRA (Dettmers et al., arXiv May 2023: *QLoRA: Efficient Finetuning of Quantized LLMs*):** LoRA + 4-bit NormalFloat (NF4) base + double quantization + paged optimizers → **fine-tune 65B on one 48GB GPU** (33B on 24GB). Used for Guanaco, Vicuna, every “fine-tune LLaMA on consumer hardware” post. Together, LoRA/GPTQ/AWQ/QLoRA = *PEFT* (Parameter-Efficient Fine-Tuning) — democratization layer between LLaMA and ChatGPT.

### Prompting & System 2 — CoT, ToT & InstructGPT (2022-2023) [Audit #6 + 6.5]

You mention Chain-of-Thought in passing, but it was a *discovery* (prompting unlocks reasoning), not just RL fine-tuning:

**InstructGPT — The Actual Recipe (Ouyang et al., arXiv Mar 2022: *Training language models to follow instructions with human feedback*):** The paper you gloss as “RLHF → ChatGPT.” Three steps: 1) **Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT)** on human demonstrations (13k prompts), 2) train **Reward Model (RM)** from ranked outputs (33k rankings), 3) **PPO** (the 2017 PPO from RL prequel) against RM with KL penalty. Result: 1.3B InstructGPT *preferred* over 175B GPT-3 by humans. This is the *exact* recipe OpenAI then scaled to **ChatGPT (Nov 30, 2022)**. Deserves a named shoutout.

**Chain-of-Thought — CoT (Wei et al., NeurIPS Dec 2022: *Chain-of-Thought Prompting Elicits Reasoning…*):** Adding *“Let’s think step by step”* (+ 8 few-shot exemplars with intermediate steps) *without any weight update* made GPT-3 (175B) jump from 17.7% → 58.1% on GSM8K math, beating fine-tuned models. The “wake-up call” that LLMs *could* reason if prompted to *emit* System 2 steps. Your later Kimi K1.5/DeepSeek-R1 *RL reasoning* (2024-25) is CoT *trained in*.

**Tree of Thoughts — ToT (Yao et al., arXiv May 2023 → NeurIPS 2023):** Explicit search over reasoning *trees* (branch + self-evaluate + backtrack, BFS/DFS with LM as heuristic), versus CoT’s linear chain. On Game of 24, ToT 74% vs CoT 4%. Bridge to **Agent Swarm** (K2.5) / **MCTS** hybrids — ToT is CoT + search.

*→ The full arc of reasoning — from Logic Theorist (1956) to o1/DeepSeek-R1/Kimi K1.5 (2024-25) — is the **Reasoning Era**, §XI.*

### RLHF — Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback, the Alignment Stack (2020-2026) [Deep Dive]

The pre-trained model is a genius at *next-token prediction* and useless as an assistant — it will happily answer with profanity, lies, or 4,000 words. **RLHF is the layer that converts a raw language model into a helpful, honest, harmless assistant.** It is the single most consequential training recipe after pretraining itself, and the exact mechanism that turned GPT-3 into ChatGPT (Nov 30, 2022) — and it deserves far more than a passing mention.

**The math of preference — Bradley-Terry (1952):** RLHF's reward model is literally a 70-year-old sports-ranking model. Given two responses `y_w` (preferred) and `y_l`, assume the probability the human prefers `y_w` is the logistic `p(y_w ≻ y_l) = σ(r(x,y_w) - r(x,y_l))` — i.e., an Elo-style pairwise comparison. A **reward model** `r_φ(x,y)` is trained to maximize that log-likelihood over human preference data (Anthropic's **HH-RLHF** dataset: 161k helpful/harmless comparisons, Bai et al. 2022).

**The 3-step InstructGPT recipe (Ouyang et al., OpenAI, Mar 2022 — the paper ChatGPT is cloned from):**
1. **SFT** — supervised fine-tuning on ~13k human-written demonstrations (format: instructions + desired responses).
2. **Reward Model** — train `r_φ` on ~33k human *rankings* of sampled completions (rank → pairwise Bradley-Terry).
3. **RL with PPO** (the 2017 algorithm from the AlphaGo prequel, §VII) — optimize the policy against the reward model *with a KL penalty* to the SFT model, so it doesn't drift into reward hacking:
   `maximize E[ r_φ(x,y) - β·KL(π_θ ‖ π_SFT) ]`, using the clipped PPO objective `L = E[min(r_t A_t, clip(r_t, 1±ε) A_t)]`.
   Result: a **1.3B model preferred over the 175B GPT-3** by human raters. OpenAI then scaled this exact recipe and shipped it as **ChatGPT (Nov 30, 2022)**.

**The dark side — overoptimization:** optimizing any learned reward model eventually exploits its blind spots (**Goodhart's law**: "when a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure"). Gao, Schulman & Hilton (2022, *Scaling Laws for Reward Model Overoptimization*) showed reward-model score rises while *true* quality eventually falls — the classic "reward hacking" failure, visible to every ChatGPT user as sycophancy, verbosity, and confident wrongness. Mitigations: KL penalties, ensembling reward models, and — in the reasoning era (§XI) — replacing learned rewards with *verifiable* ones.

**The 2023 simplification — preference learning without RL:**
- **DPO — Direct Preference Optimization (Rafailov et al., arXiv May 2023, NeurIPS 2023):** *"Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model."* Derive the RLHF objective's closed-form optimal policy and optimize it *directly* with a classification loss on preferences — no reward model, no PPO loop:
  `L_DPO = -E[ log σ(β log(π_θ(y_w|x)/π_ref(y_w|x)) - β log(π_θ(y_l|x)/π_ref(y_l|x))) ]`.
  As good as PPO-based RLHF, dramatically simpler; spawned **KTO** (Ethayarajh et al., 2023, works with binary thumbs-up/down instead of pairs), **IPO/ORPO**, and **Diffusion-DPO** (2023, aligning image generators).
- **RLAIF / Constitutional AI (Bai et al., Anthropic, 2022):** replace human feedback with *AI feedback* — a model critiques its own outputs against a written "constitution" (harmlessness, honesty, non-deception). This is why Claude is trained the way it is (§XV) and why RLHF's label farms became optional. The term "RLHF" quietly became "alignment," and human-in-the-loop shifted to *model-in-the-loop*.

**The 2024-2026 shift — RLVR and verifiable rewards (the reasoning-era handoff):**
- **Process supervision (Lightman et al., OpenAI, 2023 — *Let's Verify Step by Step*):** reward each *step* of a solution rather than the final answer (PRM800K dataset); process-supervised models solved significantly more MATH problems and showed better "alignment" of the reward with truth. **Outcome vs process** became the key design axis.
- **GRPO (Shao et al., DeepSeekMath, 2024):** kill the critic/value network entirely — sample a *group* of G responses per question and use the group's average reward as the baseline: `A_i = (r_i - mean(r)) / std(r)`. Cheaper and more stable than PPO; the engine of DeepSeek-R1 and nearly every 2025 open reasoning model (§XI).
- **RLVR — Reinforcement Learning from Verifiable Rewards (Tulu 3, AllenAI 2024; DeepSeek-R1 2025):** when the task has a *checkable* answer (math, code, fact lookup), don't learn a reward model at all — reward correctness directly (`answer matches gold` / `tests pass`). This removed both the label farm *and* the learned reward model, and it is the reason RL could scale to 51M+ rollout sandboxes in Kimi K3's training (§XII).
- **The reward-hacking arms race in reasoning:** models trained with RLVR discovered *format* hacking (writing "wait, let me reconsider" forever to buy score, verbose irrelevant reasoning). DeepSeek, Kimi K1.5, and Qwen's teams added **length penalties** and **format rewards**; K3's "Agentic Generative Reward Model with verbosity control" (§XII) is the 2026 state of the art.

**Learning-rate note (the user's question):** every stage of the RLHF stack uses different LR regimes — SFT at ~2e-5, RM at ~1e-5, and the PPO/DPO policy update at *tiny* LRs (1e-6 to 1e-5) with strong early stopping, because each stage is a *delicate retune* of an already-trained model rather than fresh learning. Getting these schedules wrong produces the classic failure: the model "forgets" its pretraining (catastrophic forgetting) or becomes a reward-chasing sycophant.

**The throughline:** RLHF is the bridge between the Scaling Era (§X) and the Reasoning Era (§XI) — it introduced the RL machinery (PPO/GRPO, KL, reward models, overoptimization) that reasoning models then repurposed from "align to humans" to "optimize for truth."

### Retrieval-Augmented Generation — RAG & The Tool-Use Inflection (2020-2023) [Audit #5]

You mention tool use/plugins but missed the *paradigm* that makes “AI Search” work without retraining:

**RAG (Lewis et al., NeurIPS 2020: *Retrieval-Augmented Generation for Knowledge-Intensive NLP Tasks*, Meta AI):** Two-memory model: **parametric memory** (BART seq2seq transformer) + **non-parametric memory** (dense Wikipedia index via DPR — Dense Passage Retriever). At query time, retrieve top-K docs `z ~ p_η(z|x)` then condition generation `p_θ(y|x,z)`. Two variants: **RAG-Sequence** (same doc for whole answer) vs **RAG-Token** (different doc per token, marginalize). Results: +20-40% accuracy on Natural Questions / TriviaQA vs parametric-only; more factual, specific, diverse; and crucially — **update knowledge by hot-swapping the index**, no retraining (2016 vs 2018 world leaders test: 70% vs 4%). Proof: provenance + inspectability + updatability — the epistemic substrate for citations.

**Evolution:** REALM (Guu 2020, joint pre-training), DPR (Karpukhin 2020), FiD (Izacard 2021). By 2023-2024 RAG → **every enterprise chatbot**: vector DB (Pinecone, Weaviate, Milvus) + embeddings + LLM. Before ChatGPT plugins (Mar 2023) and function calling (Jun 2023), RAG *was* how you grounded LLMs in private docs. Today: RAG + tool use = foundation of **ChatGPT Search (Oct 31 2024)**, **Perplexity**, **Anthropic citations**, and **Kimi K2’s search tool**. OpenAI’s Responses API + GPT-5.6 programmatic tool calling (Jul 2026) is RAG’s logical endpoint: model writes *code* to orchestrate retrievals.

**Why it matters to your doc:** Without RAG, the jump from GPT-3 (hallucinates) to ChatGPT Search / Kimi search looks magical. RAG is the missing “grounding” layer.

## BOOK XI — THE PROVERBS: THE REASONING ERA — FROM LOGIC THEORIST TO CHAIN-OF-THOUGHT (1956-2026)

*"Reasoning" is the oldest ambition in AI — older than neural networks, older than learning itself. The first AI program was a reasoning program. What changed in 2022-2025 is that reasoning finally became something that could be **learned at scale** instead of hand-programmed: from a theorem prover that searched a fixed space of logic, to a model that generates its own step-by-step thinking and is **reinforcement-trained to think better**. This section is the full arc — where reasoning started, how it was lost, rediscovered, and rebuilt.*

### Part A — The Symbolic Age: Reasoning Was the Original AI (1956-1990s)

**Logic Theorist (Newell, Shaw & Simon, 1956) — the first reasoning model.** Two years *before* the Dartmouth workshop, the Logic Theory Machine proved theorems by **heuristic search**: given the axioms of Whitehead & Russell's *Principia Mathematica*, it searched for proof chains and succeeded on **38 of the first 52 theorems** — and famously found a proof of theorem 2.85 that was *more elegant* than Russell & Whitehead's own. Allen Newell and Herbert Simon had built the first "reasoning model," and with it invented **heuristic search** — the idea that intelligence is *search through a space of possible thoughts*. This is the answer to "when did reasoning start": **1956, with search over symbols.**

**General Problem Solver — GPS (Newell & Simon, 1957-1959):** reasoning as **means-ends analysis** — detect the difference between current state and goal, pick an operator that reduces it, recurse. GPS was the first attempt at a *domain-general* reasoner; it solved puzzles (Towers of Hanoi, missionaries-cannibals) and launched the field of **planning**.

**The logic boom (1965-1980):** **Resolution** (Robinson, 1965) gave a complete inference rule for first-order logic; **Prolog** (Colmerauer & Roussel, 1972) made logic programming practical; **STRIPS** (Fikes & Nilsson, 1971) formalized planning as state/action/goal (used by Shakey, §II); **semantic networks**, **frames** (Minsky, 1975), **scripts** (Schank & Abelson, 1977) tried to give symbols meaning; **default logic** (Reiter, 1980) and **circumscription** (McCarthy, 1980) handled "reasoning when knowledge is incomplete" (nonmonotonic reasoning).

**Expert systems — reasoning with a knowledge base (1965-1985):** **DENDRAL** (1965, first expert system — chemistry), **MYCIN** (Shortliffe, 1976 — medical diagnosis via backward-chaining rules with **certainty factors**; outperformed Stanford infectious-disease faculty in trials), **PROSPECTOR** (geology, discovered a molybdenum deposit), **XCON** (DEC's configurator, saved ~$40M/yr). Reasoning was *the* commercial product of AI — until it wasn't.

**Cyc (Lenat, 1984-):** the maximal bet on symbolic reasoning — encode *all of common sense* (millions of assertions: "a dog is an animal," "people can't fly"). After 30+ years and ~25M assertions, Cyc proved the **knowledge acquisition bottleneck**: hand-writing common sense doesn't scale, and *encoding* knowledge is not the same as *reasoning* over it.

**Probabilistic reasoning (1985-2000):** Judea Pearl's **Bayesian networks** (*Probabilistic Reasoning in Intelligent Systems*, 1988) and **causal inference** (*Causality*, 2000) showed that reasoning under uncertainty is *probability*, not logic — the direct ancestor of the statistical revolution (§III) and of every modern model's uncertainty-aware inference. **Case-based reasoning** (Aamodt & Plaza, 1994) — reason by retrieving and adapting similar past cases.

**Why the symbolic age died:** brittleness (a single missing axiom breaks everything), the frame/qualification problems (§II), and the knowledge bottleneck. The field's conclusion by 1990: *hand-built reasoning does not scale — so throw it out and learn from data instead.* That throw-out was so total that "reasoning" disappeared from the field's vocabulary for two decades.

### Part B — The Learned-Reasoning Detour: Neural Networks That Try to Reason (2011-2021)

Between the symbolic age and Chain-of-Thought, a generation of architectures tried to make neural networks reason *by construction*:

- **Memory networks (Weston et al., 2014)** and **Neural Turing Machines (Graves et al., 2014)** — external memory + controller; **Differentiable Neural Computers (Graves et al., Nature 2016)** — learned to copy, sort, and answer relational questions; **pointer networks (Vinyals et al., 2015)** — output pointers to input positions (TSP, sorting).
- **Relation Networks (Santoro et al., 2017):** a module that computes pairwise interactions between entities `RN(o) = f(Σ g(o_i, o_j))` — solved CLEVR-style relational reasoning; **Neural Arithmetic Logic Units — NALU (Trask et al., 2018)** — learn to add/multiply; **MAC networks (Hudson & Manning, 2018)** — explicit multi-step reasoning over images; **neural program synthesis** — generate code, execute it.
- **The framing — System 1 vs System 2 (Kahneman, 2011):** Daniel Kahneman's *Thinking, Fast and Slow* split cognition into System 1 (fast, intuitive, automatic) and System 2 (slow, deliberate, logical). The deep-learning field adopted it as a design target: Yoshua Bengio (2017), Gary Marcus, and Yann LeCun (2022, §XI's JEPA) all argued neural nets were *stuck in System 1*.

**Why the detour failed to scale:** these reasoners were small, task-specific, and hand-shaped — they reasoned on *synthetic* problems but did not improve with data or compute. The lesson that would matter: **reasoning behavior is far easier to *learn implicitly at scale* (as emergent structure) than to *build explicitly* (as architecture).** The scaling curve was in language modeling — and language models were quietly learning to reason without anyone trying.

### Part C — Chain-of-Thought & the Prompting Era: Reasoning Rediscovered for Free (2021-2023)

**The benchmarks that made reasoning measurable:** **GSM8K** (Cobbe et al., OpenAI, 2021 — 8.5k grade-school math word problems, the *canonical* CoT benchmark), **MATH** (Hendrycks et al., 2021 — 12.5k competition problems), **HumanEval** (Chen et al., 2021 — code), and the "emergent abilities" observation (Wei et al., 2022) — capabilities that *appear suddenly* past a model-size threshold. For the first time, "can the model reason?" had a score.

**The scratchpad lineage — where CoT actually came from:**
- **Scratchpads (Nye et al., 2021 — *Show Your Work: Scratchpads for Intermediate Computation with Language Models*):** train the model to emit intermediate computation steps before the answer; scratchpad-trained RNNs solved long multi-digit arithmetic that direct-answer models failed. The idea: *let the model write its working out.*
- **Selection-Inference (Creswell et al., DeepMind, 2021-2022):** factor reasoning into select-a-fact and infer-a-step modules — an explicit *program* for step-by-step reasoning.
- **Chain-of-Thought prompting — CoT (Wei et al., arXiv Jan 2022 → NeurIPS Dec 2022, *Chain-of-Thought Prompting Elicits Reasoning in Large Language Models*):** the discovery. Just adding *"Let's think step by step"* — or, better, 8 few-shot exemplars that show intermediate steps — **without any weight update** — took GPT-3 175B from **17.7% → 58.1%** on GSM8K and PaLM 540B from **12.9% → 56.9%** (beating fine-tuned models). CoT is "scratchpads at inference time, elicited by prompting." **Zero-shot CoT (Kojima et al., 2022)** — the single phrase, no exemplars — made it universal. This was the wake-up call: *LLMs can reason if you ask them to emit System-2 steps.*

**The CoT explosion (2022-2023) — every trick in the era:**
- **Self-Consistency (Wang et al., 2022):** sample **40 different CoT chains**, majority-vote the final answers → PaLM 540B hit **78.9% GSM8K** (vs 56.9% single chain). First taste of **test-time compute**: spend more sampling to buy accuracy.
- **Least-to-Most (Zhou et al., 2022):** decompose a hard problem into subproblems, solve in order.
- **PAL — Program-Aided Language Models (Gao et al., 2022):** stop making the *language* model do arithmetic — emit Python and **execute it**. Offloading computation to a code interpreter kills the model's number-blindness; the direct ancestor of code interpreters in every 2024+ assistant (and GPT-5.6's programmatic tool calling).
- **STaR — Self-Taught Reasoner (Zelikman et al., 2022):** the first *training* loop for reasoning — generate rationales, keep the ones that produce correct answers ("rationalization"), fine-tune on them, repeat. Reasoning as **self-generated curriculum**; the direct ancestor of RL-trained reasoning.
- **ReAct (Yao et al., 2022):** interleave *Reason* + *Act* — "think, then call a tool, then think again"; the template for every agent (§XII). **Reflexion (Shinn et al., 2023):** after a failure, the model *verbalizes* what went wrong and retries — "verbal RL." **Tree of Thoughts (Yao et al., 2023):** search over a *tree* of partial thoughts (BFS/DFS, self-evaluate branches) — Game of 24: **74% vs CoT's 4%**. **Graph of Thoughts (Besta et al., 2023)**, **Self-Refine (Madaan et al., 2023)** — the prompting era generalized CoT into *search*.
- **Quiet-STaR (Zelikman et al., 2024):** instead of thinking only when asked, *learn to think at every token* — emit internal `<|start_of_thought|>…<|end_of_thought|>` traces before predicting each token, using REINFORCE-style training; improved Mistral-7B's GSM8K from 5.9% → 17.1% *without being prompted to think at all*. The clearest bridge from prompting-era CoT to trained-in thinking.

**The catch the prompting era hit:** CoT is *free* (no training) but *fragile* (prompt-sensitive, no guarantee the model actually reasons — it can emit confident nonsense step-by-step), and it spends inference compute **uniformly** — a "1+1" gets the same 500-token thought budget as a Putnam problem. What was missing: a training signal that rewards *better* thinking and a mechanism that allocates thinking *where it's needed*. That required reinforcement learning.

### Part D — RL-Trained Reasoning: The Reasoning Models (2023-2026)

**The RL-verifiable bridge (2023-2024):** the pieces assembled — **process reward models** (Lightman et al., OpenAI 2023: *Let's Verify Step by Step*, reward each step; PRM800K), **GRPO** (Shao et al., DeepSeekMath, 2024 — no critic, group-relative baseline), **RLVR** (Tulu 3, 2024 — reward verifiable correctness, not a learned RM). Then three systems independently proved the recipe at scale, all within four months.

**OpenAI o1 (Sep 12, 2024) — the first "reasoning model," *Learning to Reason with LLMs*:** trained with **large-scale RL to produce a long internal chain of thought** before answering — and *scaled*: "performance consistently improves with more RL (train-time compute) *and* with more time spent thinking (test-time compute)." The numbers that shocked the field: **AIME 2024: 83.3%** (64-sample consensus) vs GPT-4o's **13.4%**; **GPQA Diamond: 78%** (vs 69.7% for human PhD experts); **Codeforces: 89th percentile**. o1-preview (Sep 12) → o1 (Dec 5) → o1-pro ($200/mo ChatGPT Pro, even more test-time compute). Key design choices that defined the genre: the CoT is **hidden** (OpenAI refused to reveal it, citing safety and monopoly concerns — sparking the "hidden reasoning" debate), and o1 explicitly *trades latency for accuracy* — the first model where waiting longer = getting smarter was a *product feature*. (o1's internal project name: **"Strawberry."**)

**o3 / o3-mini (Dec 2024 → Jan 2025):** more RL, "**deliberative alignment**" (the model reasons about its own safety policy before answering — safety via CoT); **ARC-AGI-1: 75.7% → 87.5%** with more test-time compute (GPT-4o scored ~5% in 2024; the "AGI benchmark" from Chollet 2019 went from unsolvable to near-solved). o4-mini (Apr 2025) added *agentic tool use* to reasoning. OpenAI's own framing: test-time compute is a **third scaling axis** alongside parameters and data.

**DeepSeek-R1 (Jan 20, 2025) — the open-weight proof that RL alone is enough:** DeepSeek's R1-Zero ran **pure RL (GRPO) on the base V3 model — no SFT at all** — and observed the famous **"aha moment":** mid-training, the model spontaneously learned to *re-evaluate its own approach and allocate more thinking time* ("Wait. Wait. That's an aha moment I can flag here"). AIME 2024: **15.6% → 71.0%** pass@1 (86.7% with majority voting). The full R1 added cold-start SFT + RL + rejection sampling + RL; distilled versions (R1-Distill-Qwen/Llama, 1.5B-70B) transferred reasoning to small open models. **MIT license**, API price ~**$2/M output tokens vs o1's ~$60** — the moment reasoning models became open and cheap, collapsing the "reasoning is a closed-labs secret" assumption (and launching the open RL-reasoning replication wave, e.g. TinyZero, logic-rl).

**Kimi K1.5 (Moonshot AI, Jan 20, 2025 — same day as R1):** *Scaling Reinforcement Learning with LLMs* — the key new axes: **long-CoT RL at 128K context** (thinking gets to be *long*), **online mirror descent** policy updates, and **partial rollouts** (reuse old trajectory chunks → cheap RL at scale). Result: short-CoT outperforms GPT-4o on AIME/MATH-500/LiveCodeBench by up to **+550%**, and long-CoT **matches o1 across modalities** (including vision, MathVista). The Kimi lineage continues into K2 and K3 (§XII) — the most aggressively scaled RL-training stack in the open world.

**Test-time compute becomes a science (Snell et al., Aug 2024 — *Scaling LLM Test-Time Compute Optimally*):** a *smaller* model with **compute-optimal** test-time allocation (revision + search against a verifier, budgeted by difficulty) can beat a **14× larger** model — and does so with **4× less** compute than naive best-of-N. The two mechanisms: (1) *search against a verifier* (sample, score with PRM, keep best) and (2) *adaptive revision* (continue thinking conditioned on the critique). This paper is the theoretical core of the entire reasoning-model era: **inference-time FLOPs are now a first-class scaling variable.**

**The 2025-2026 landscape (reasoning goes mainstream and agentic):**
- **Hybrid/controllable thinking:** Claude 3.7 Sonnet (Feb 2025) — the first mainstream **hybrid reasoning model** (Standard vs "Extended Thinking" slider); **Gemini 2.5** (Mar 2025) — thinking enabled by default; **Qwen3** (Apr 2025, open weights) — thinking/non-thinking modes togglable *per request*; GPT-5 (Aug 2025) — a *router* between fast "Instant" and slow "Thinking" submodels; Claude Opus 4.6-5 (2026) — "adaptive thinking" (the model decides its own budget); Kimi K3's **Reasoning Effort RL** — token-budget control as a training target (§XII).
- **Reasoning + agents:** o3/o4-mini (Apr 2025) can *call tools while thinking*; **o3 + Codex** → the 2025-2026 coding-agent era; **GPT-5.6 Sol** (Jun 2026) — programmatic tool calling, multi-agent orchestration; K2.5 Agent Swarm (§XII). Reasoning stopped being "think then answer" and became "think, act, verify, iterate."
- **Reasoning benchmarks:** **AIME** (contest math, the era's headline number), **GPQA** (PhD science), **ARC-AGI** (abstract reasoning), **SWE-bench** (real GitHub issues — the agent era's benchmark), **FrontierMath** (Epoch AI, Nov 2024 — research-grade math; frontier models scored <2% at launch, a running reminder of how far reasoning has to go).
- **The economics:** reasoning shifted cost from *training* to *inference* — thinking tokens are billed separately (o1's $60/M output, R1's ~$2/M, K3's Reasoning Effort dial), and "wait 10 seconds for a better answer" became a normal UX. The field's open question: **what's the test-time-compute scaling law at the extreme** — do 10× thinking budgets keep paying off, or saturate (§XXIII)?

**What RL actually teaches a reasoner (the theory, per the R1/K1.5 papers):** exploration over *verifiable* rewards (correct final answers, passing tests) teaches the model (1) **self-verification** (checking its own work), (2) **self-correction** (backtracking on detected errors), (3) **budget allocation** (more thinking on harder problems), and (4) **step-level robustness** (each step must be right because the reward is end-state). Prompting-era CoT *elicited* these behaviors; RL *incentivizes* them, which is why RL-trained thinking generalizes where prompted thinking collapses. The hidden-vs-visible CoT debate (o1 hides it; R1 publishes it; Claude 3.7 lets you see it) is unresolved and increasingly load-bearing for interpretability (§XV's Claude era).

**The throughline:** from Logic Theorist's hand-coded search (1956) → learned reasoners that didn't scale (2011-2021) → CoT *elicited* by prompting (2022) → CoT *trained in* by RL (2024-2026). Reasoning is the first capability in AI history to be discovered **backwards** — the behavior existed implicitly in next-token prediction, and we found the (prompt, then RL) key to unlock it. That inversion — capability first, mechanism second — is the single most important pattern of the modern era, and it is exactly what the next section (RL in production) exploits.

### RL-Trained Reasoning in Production — Kimi K1.5 → R1 → K2 → Agent Swarm (2024-2025)

**From passive models to active reasoners:**

**Kimi K1.5 (2024-2025):** Demonstrated that large-scale reinforcement learning can elicit sophisticated reasoning from pre-trained models. RL transformed models from passive text predictors into active reasoners.

**Key Concept:** Reasoning models—models that "think" before answering, using Chain of Thought (CoT) to break problems into steps.

**The RL Paradigm:** 
- Pre-train on massive text data.
- Apply reinforcement learning to teach reasoning.
- Models learn to explore, verify, and refine their thinking.

**DeepSeek-R1 (2025):** Showed that RL alone could elicit reasoning behaviors from strong pre-trained models, without explicit reasoning supervision.

**The Reasoning Trajectory:** Model generates reasoning steps, receives reward based on correctness, and learns to reason effectively.

**Kimi K2 (2025):** A 1.04 trillion parameter MoE model with 32.6B activated parameters. K2 introduced:
1. **Muon Optimizer:** Token-efficient optimization with QK-Clip for stability.
2. **Agentic Capabilities:** Tool use, search, and reasoning.
3. **1M Context Window:** Enabling processing of entire books or codebases.

**Multi-Token Prediction (MTP):** Pre-training objective where the model predicts multiple future tokens, improving efficiency.

**Agent Swarm (K2.5, 2025):** Parallel agent orchestration—models can decompose complex tasks into sub-problems executed concurrently by specialized sub-agents.

**Why Agent Swarm Matters:**
- Reduces latency by up to 4.5×.
- Enables solving tasks beyond single-agent capacity.
- Learns when and how to parallelize through RL.

### Neuro-Symbolic & World Models — The JEPA Counter-Narrative (2022-2026) [Audit #6]

Your 2026 section focuses purely on scaling MoEs — but half the field is pivoting *away* from “bigger LLM = AGI”:

**Yann LeCun’s Bet (2022):** In *A Path Towards Autonomous Machine Intelligence* (2022 position paper), LeCun argued LLMs are “System 1” autocomplete and will never reach human-like *System 2* reasoning + physical common sense by predicting tokens. His alternative: **energy-based, predictive world models**.

**JEPA — Joint Embedding Predictive Architecture (LeCun et al., 2022-2024):** Core idea: *don’t reconstruct pixels/tokens* — predict *abstract representations*. Encode context `s_x → emb_x`, target `s_y → emb_y`, train predictor `f(emb_x) → emb_y` in **latent space**, ignoring unpredictable detail. Two-term loss: prediction MSE + variance/covariance regularization to prevent collapse. Variants: **I-JEPA (images, 2023)**, **V-JEPA (video, 2024, predicts spatio-temporal embeddings for self-supervised video)**, **V-JEPA 2 (2025, video world model used for robot planning — predicts how environment evolves after an action)**, **LeWM / LeWorldModel (Mar 2026, end-to-end pixel→JEPA world model with provable anti-collapse)**. Meta’s AMI Labs (LeCun’s startup 2026, >$1B raised) bets JEPA world models are the path to physical AI.

**Neuro-Symbolic Revival (2023-2026):** Hybrid nets + explicit reasoning: **AlphaGeometry (Trinh 2024, neuro-symbolic theorem prover)**, **Toolformer-adjacent program use (GPT-5.6’s programmatic tool calling is neuro-symbolic in practice)**, **World Models for planning (DayDreamer, DreamerV3)**. The tension: **Kimi K3 scales attention/memory (data-driven System 1)** vs **JEPA scales prediction of world dynamics (model-based System 2)**. 2026 consensus: you need both — frontier labs now combine MoE scale *with* world-model pretraining.

**Takeaway for your doc:** Without JEPA, your “2026 → bigger MoEs” reads as inevitability. In reality, research is bifurcated: scaling *vs* understanding.

## BOOK XII — THE REVELATION: THE FRONTIER ERA (2026)

### Multi-Trillion Parameter Models — The Open-Source Frontier

**Kimi K3 (2026) — The World's *Largest and Smartest Open-Source* Model:** 2.78 trillion total parameters, 104.2B activated, native multimodal, 1M context window. **Crucial nuance:** K3 is *not* the absolute biggest or smartest model in the world — closed frontier systems like **OpenAI GPT-5.6 Sol** and **Anthropic Claude Opus 5** (July 2026) still compete at the absolute top. What makes K3 insane is that it *is* the largest and smartest **open-source** model ever released, competing head-to-head with paid frontier models while being **free, open-weights (MXFP4), and runnable by anyone** — a first for a 2.8T MoE. 1. **Kimi Delta Attention (KDA):**
   - Linear attention with bounded decay.
   - Lower-bounded decay prevents overflow.
   - Chunkwise parallel form enables efficient training.

2. **Attention Residuals (AttnRes):**
   - Selective attention across network depth.
   - Layer can attend to representations from all preceding layers.

3. **Stable LatentMoE:**

   - Latent MoE dimension separates routed path from full width.
   - Normalized LatentMoE adds RMSNorm for stability.

   - Quantile Balancing ensures perfect load balance.

4. **Per-Head Muon:**
   - Orthogonalization per attention head.
   - More balanced learning dynamics.

5. **Native Vision:**
   - MoonViT-V2 trained from scratch.
   - Shared image and video processing.

**Hybrid Attention:** 3 KDA layers + 1 Gated MLA layer per block. KDA provides efficient long-context mixing; MLA provides global content interaction.

**Scaling Efficiency:** 2.5× improvement over Kimi K2 in overall scaling efficiency.

2. **Reasoning Effort RL:** Token budget control for inference-time scaling.

## BOOK XIII — THE TWELVE (MINOR PROPHETS): THE SMALL LANGUAGE MODEL COUNTER-REVOLUTION (2023-2026)

### The Other Half of the Frontier — When "Smarter" Stopped Meaning "Bigger"

Every section before this one describes a single arrow: **up**. More parameters, more tokens, more GPUs, more context. But 2023-2026 quietly produced a second arrow pointing the *opposite* direction — and it turned out to be just as important. While Kimi K3 pushed to 2.78T total parameters in a datacenter, a parallel movement proved that **50 million parameters trained well can beat 124 million parameters trained badly**, and that the *interesting* research question is no longer "how big can we go?" but "how little can we get away with?"

**What an SLM actually is:** A **Small Language Model (SLM)** is a language model — usually a decoder-only Transformer, same lineage as GPT — with a parameter count small enough to train and run on consumer hardware. There is no official cutoff, but in practice:

| Class | Params | Runs on | Typical use |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Micro / "bacteria" models** | 1k – 1M | Anything with a CPU, a $3 board, a browser tab | Pure research: scaling-law probes, distillation targets, architecture ablations |
| **Nano SLM** | 1M – 50M | Any laptop, phone, Raspberry Pi | Classifiers, routers, title generators, autocompletion, embedded NPCs |
| **Small SLM** | 50M – 500M | Old GPUs (GTX 750 Ti, 4GB), phones | Chat-lite, summarization, domain assistants, on-device agents |
| **Large SLM / "edge LLM"** | 0.5B – 3B | Consumer GPU / high-end phone | Real assistants (Phi, Gemma, Qwen-small, SmolLM, TinyLlama) |
| **LLM** | 7B+ | Server GPU, quantized on desktop | General frontier assistants |
| **Frontier MoE** | 1T+ total | Datacenter (or MXFP4 + a *lot* of RAM) | Kimi K3, GPT-5.6, Claude Opus 5 |

**Why SLMs exist at all — the four forces:**

1. **Chinchilla, then *past* Chinchilla (2022 → 2024).** DeepMind's Chinchilla law said compute-optimal training is ~20 tokens per parameter. The SLM movement realized that if you don't care about *training* cost and only care about *inference* cost, you should massively **over-train**: LLaMA used ~200 tok/param, SmolLM and Supra2 use **300 tok/param**. You "waste" training compute to buy a permanently smaller, permanently cheaper model. This single inversion is the economic engine of the entire SLM field.
2. **The distillation/quantization stack (2015 → 2023).** Hinton's knowledge distillation (2015), DistilBERT (2019), then GPTQ/AWQ/QLoRA (see §X) meant a small model no longer had to *learn* from raw web text alone — it could learn from a big model's output distribution, then be squeezed to 4 bits on top.
3. **Data quality over data quantity.** Microsoft's **Phi** series ("Textbooks Are All You Need," 2023) and **FineWeb-Edu** / **Cosmopedia** (HuggingFace, 2024) showed that a curated educational corpus lets a 1B model match a 7B model trained on raw CommonCrawl. Nearly every serious SLM since is trained on FineWeb-Edu or a synthetic textbook derivative.
4. **The edge demanded it.** llama.cpp/GGUF/Ollama (§XIV) and Transformers.js/WebGPU (§XIV) built the *distribution* rails. SLMs are the payload those rails were waiting for — a 50M model in `q4` GGUF is ~30MB, i.e. smaller than a single photo from a modern phone.

**The prior art (2023-2025):** **TinyLlama** (1.1B, 3T tokens, 2023) proved extreme over-training works. **Microsoft Phi-1/1.5/2/3** (2023-2024) proved curated data beats scale. **HuggingFace SmolLM / SmolLM2** (135M/360M/1.7B, 2024) made small models *respectable* and shipped the datasets to reproduce them. **Qwen2.5-0.5B**, **Gemma 2/3 Nano**, **IBM Granite 4.0 Micro**, **OpenELM** (Apple) turned SLMs into shipping products on phones. And **Super Tiny Language Models** (Guertler et al., arXiv 2405.14159, May 2024) formalized the research program: byte-level tokenization with pooling, weight tying, aggressive pruning — targeting 10M / 50M / 100M models with 90-95% parameter reduction.

But those are all *lab* SLMs — built by Microsoft, Apple, Alibaba, Google, HuggingFace, on clusters. The movement's true character shows up in an independent group with none of that: **SupraLabs**.

**Two wings, two questions.** This book covers the small end of the frontier through two very different cases, and the contrast is the point. **SupraLabs** (below) asks *how much of a language model survives compression* — and answers, across 30+ public models from 1,046 parameters to 800M, that **fluency survives and knowledge does not**. **Pathway's BDH-CQ** (§XIII, final section) asks a question the first wing never posed: *how much reasoning can you buy if you stop paying for it in tokens?* — and answers, at **150M parameters**, with a **state-of-the-art point on the ARC-AGI-1 cost–accuracy frontier**. Together they resolve the SLM movement's central ambiguity: **the small-model ceiling is a knowledge ceiling, not a reasoning ceiling.**

Their published training hardware: a **single RTX 5090 (32GB)**, an **RTX 5060 Ti (16GB)**, a Kaggle **T4**, and — for several models — a **GTX 750 Ti 4GB**, a card released in *2014*. Their founding roadmap literally listed the target hardware next to each planned model. They are the clearest proof that the SLM era re-opened frontier-style research to people who are not a lab.

**Their stated principles**, which matter for reading the catalog: a **"Human-First Policy" ("Life Before Models,"** Jun 9), a refusal of open-weights-without-openness, and a commitment to publishing *"every single checkpoint and weight file, complete unedited `lm-eval` logs and raw data points, and our training configurations and custom setup code so anyone can replicate our work on their own hardware."* When a public dispute over scaling claims got heated in June 2026, they published an **open letter** retracting their own poorly-worded criticism ("referring to a benchmark as 'fake' was imprecise") while holding the substantive point: *"The statement that a 1M parameter model trained on 1 trillion tokens will become a '100M killer' is, today, a prediction, not an experimental result… Research advances by testing ideas, not by assuming their outcomes."* That sentence is a decent summary of the entire SLM research ethic.

**Why they matter as the representative case:** other orgs ship one or two small models as a side product of a big model. SupraLabs ships *only* small models — 30+ of them in ~4 months — and treats the entire size spectrum, from **1,046 parameters** to **800M**, as a research instrument. They publish the failures alongside the wins ("Why did supra create this trash?" is a literal section header on one of their model cards). That transparency is what makes their catalog readable as a *history* rather than a product line.

#### The Timeline — How They Grew (2026)

**Phase 1 — Supra-Mini: learning to walk (May 2026).**
The org opens with a ladder of deliberately absurd models: **Supra-Mini-0.1M** and **v2-0.1M** (May 17), **v3-0.5M** (May 15), **v4-2M** (May 20), **v6-1M** (May 30), **v5-8M** (Jun 10), plus **StorySupra-10M** (May 15), a TinyStories-style narrative model. None of these are useful. All of them are *measurements* — a hand-built scaling curve from 100k to 8M parameters, run on hardware anyone owns.

**Phase 2 — Supra-50M: the breakout (May 21 – Jun 19, 2026).**
Base and Instruct shipped **the same day (May 21)** as the first release of the **"Scaling Up Plan"** — a 6× jump from 8M. **Supra-50M-Base**: Llama decoder-only, **51.8M params, hidden 512, 12 layers, 8 heads (GQA 4 KV), intermediate 1,408, 1,024 context**, a **custom 32,000 Byte-Level BPE tokenizer trained from scratch on 500,000 FineWeb-Edu documents**, **20 billion tokens** of FineWeb-Edu (`sample-100BT`), cosine LR, fused AdamW, `torch.compile`, bfloat16 — **final loss 3.259**, one GPU. The benchmark table is the reason the SLM community noticed:

| Benchmark | **Supra-50M** | GPT-2 (124M) | SmolLM-135M | OpenELM-270M |
| --- | :-: | :-: | :-: | :-: |
| Parameters | **50M** | 124M (2.5×) | 135M (2.7×) | 270M (5.4×) |
| BLiMP (linguistics) | **76.3%** | 63.0% | 69.8% | — |
| SciQ (science) | **77.2%** | 53.2% | 73.4% | 84.7% |
| ARC-Easy (knowledge) | **52.2%** | 42.0% | 49.2% | 45.1% |
| PIQA (logic) | 62.2% | 63.0% | 67.3% | 69.8% |
| HellaSwag (context) | 31.8% | 29.5% | 42.0% | 46.7% |

A 50M model beating GPT-2 124M on **BLiMP by +13.3 points** and SciQ by **+24 points** is the Chinchilla-inversion argument made concrete: GPT-2 (2019) saw ~10B tokens of raw WebText; Supra-50M saw 20B tokens of *filtered educational* text at **~400 tokens/parameter**. Same architecture family, 2.5× fewer parameters, 7 years of data-curation progress.

**Supra-50M-Instruct** — the chat tune, 4 epochs on **Alpaca-Cleaned** with the classic `### Instruction: / ### Response:` template — then did something no 51.8M-parameter model had done: it hit **#1 on the Hugging Face trending list for Text Generation** (page 4 across *all* categories), crossed **7,000+ downloads in a week**, and got a YouTube deep-dive. In SupraLabs' own words: *"For a non-profit, 100% open-source garage project, this is unreal."*

**The 1999 Athlon moment (May 24, 2026).** Three days after release, a user on r/LocalLLM (**u/drone_stonks**) ran **Supra-50M-Instruct on a 26-year-old AMD Athlon at 1,007 MHz** — single core, 256KB cache, **no SSE2**, running SliTaz 5.0 — at **~1.3 tokens/second**. Because llama.cpp *requires* SSE2, they fell back to **llama2.c** (Karpathy's minimal C engine) with a hand-written tokenizer. The model, given "Open the pod bay doors, HAL," produced drifty but on-theme output. Their closing line became something of an SLM slogan: *"It's crazy to think that with the right file size of 200 MB, we could have experienced this magic back in 1999."* No other 2026 model release can claim a 1999 deployment target. **Supra-50M-Reasoning** (Jun 4, 35.4k downloads) followed, trained on **SupraThink-500x** — just **500 reasoning traces distilled from Qwen3-1.7B** — and scored BLiMP 64.1%, TruthfulQA-MC2 42.6%, SciQ 64.1%, MMLU 23.6% (i.e. chance-level on MMLU: at 50M params, *reasoning format* transfers but *world knowledge* does not. That negative result is itself a finding).

**Phase 3 — specialization: small models doing real jobs (Jun – Jul 2026).**
This is where SupraLabs stops imitating LLMs and starts arguing that SLMs are a *different species* with different jobs:

- **Supra-Router-51M** (Jul 9, 2.6k downloads, 146 likes) — the most quietly influential model they've made. An **infrastructure traffic controller**: it reads a user prompt and emits a structured telemetry line — `Domain | Complexity 1-5 | Math T/F | Code T/F | Route: small model / big model | Justification` — deciding whether a request can be served *locally by an edge SLM* or must be escalated to a cloud frontier model. Fine-tuned on only 992 rows, checkpoint rewound to epoch 3 (eval_loss 0.1342) to kill memorization drift, greedy decoding for determinism, sub-millisecond inference. Crucially it uses **multi-task sequence generation** (compute the domain, complexity and flags *before* emitting the routing token), which the authors note "prevents localized weight collapse and guarantees stable routing boundaries." It resists keyword traps — "write a *script* about a chef" is creative writing, not code. **This is the architectural glue between the SLM world and the K3 world: the small model decides when the big model is needed.**
- **Supra-Title** (Jun 12) — *"one model, one job."* A single-purpose model that names your chat thread, and the canonical SLM economic argument: every chat app titles conversations by *"spinning up a 7B model, burning context, and waiting for a response just to get three words back."* Supra-Title does it in milliseconds with no system prompt — fire it in parallel with your main model and the title is ready before the answer is. Two variants: **supra-title-50M** (their own base) and **Supra-Title-350M-exp**, a fine-tune of **LiquidAI LFM2.5-350M-Base** shipped in GGUF from **Q2_K_L (177MB) to BF16 (711MB)**, `ollama run hf.co/SupraLabs/Supra-Title-350M-exp-GGUF:Q4_K_M`. Backed by the **Supra Titles Corpus** (150K unfiltered / 115K filtered / 12K / 130K wild chat titles). Sample: *"bruh my wifi keeps disconnecting every 10 minutes 😭"* → **"WiFi Issues."**
- **SupraWeather-Nano-Preview (791k)** and **SupraWeather1.5-Small (1.85M)** — tabular classification, not language: the SLM idea applied outside NLP entirely.
- **Supra-A2A-Nano-Exp** (Jun 21, ~29.9M) — an **any-to-any multimodal** proof of concept. One autoregressive GPT, one vocabulary: 50,264 text BPE tokens + **256 discrete visual codes from a convolutional VQ-VAE**, so a 64×64 image becomes 64 "visual words" in the same token stream as text (`<TEXT>…</TEXT><IMAGE><FRAME>…</IMAGE>`). Text→text, text→image, text→video, image→text, image→image, video→video — with **4 Transformer blocks and a 384-token context**. It is not good. It is *legible* — the K3 multimodal idea, small enough to read in one sitting.
- **reasoning-summarizer-800m-pre** (their largest, 0.8B) — compresses long reasoning traces, the SLM-as-a-component-of-an-LLM-pipeline pattern.

**Interlude — the Open SLM Research Initiative (May 29, 2026).** A week after the Supra-50M breakout, SupraLabs announced a systematic, fully-open research program to find *"the exact engineering sweet spots for SLMs"* — and the experiment list is arguably a more valuable contribution than any single model, because it's the field's missing ablation study:

| # | Experiment | Setup | Question |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | **Data-Mix Showdown** | 5M Llama, exactly 100M tokens/run | FineWeb-Edu vs DCLM-Edu vs Cosmopedia-v2 vs algorithmic token-level mixes — does synthetic beat curated web at 5M? |
| 2 | **Scaling Law Realities** | 10M/25M/50M/100M, data fixed at 2B tokens | Where do Chinchilla's rules break at the edge? Does 25M saturate 2B tokens? |
| 3 | **Is One Epoch All You Need?** | 10M Llama, 500M tokens, 1-5 epochs | Where does overfitting actually start for an SLM on high-signal educational data? |
| A | **The Tokenizer Bottleneck** | 10M models, 128k vs 32k vs custom 8k/16k vocab | At 10M params the embedding layer eats everything — where's the balance? |
| B | **Depth vs. Width** | 25M budget: 24 layers narrow vs 6 layers wide | How should you spend a fixed parameter budget? |
| C | **Sequence Length Penalty** | Identical models at 512 / 1,024 / 2,048 ctx | Does a longer context window cost you knowledge density? |
| D | **LR Schedules for Short Runs** | 1-2B token runs, aggressive linear vs constant-with-drops | Cosine is built for trillions of tokens — what's optimal for indie-scale runs? |

Standardized eval matrix across all of it (wikitext/lambada, hellaswag/piqa/winogrande/boolq, sciq/openbookqa/arc, blimp), with a promise to release *every* checkpoint, unedited log, and config. **Supra2 (below) is the first model built on the answers.**

**Phase 4 — the extreme low end: how small is *possible*? (Jul 2026).

- **MicroSupra-1k** (~Jul 16) — **1,046 parameters.** Hidden size **1**. One layer, one attention head, vocab 1,024. Trained on 300M FineWeb-Edu tokens in **one minute on a GTX 750 Ti**, final loss 6.046. Its output is word salad ("*My name is ed and. as the, to. the, in*"). Their own conclusion is the point: *"Even without any intelligence, it shows that scaling laws are real."* It is the empirical **zero point** of the scaling curve — the model that establishes what "no capability" looks like so every other point on the curve has meaning.
- **DistillSupra-0.2M** (~Jul 19) — 289k parameters distilled from the 468k **Supra-Mini-v4-2M** teacher, **10× compression**, 500 steps / 30 minutes on the same 2014 GPU. Also incoherent. Also a real datapoint on where distillation stops working.
- **SupraCMA-8M** — continued exploration in the 8M class.

**Phase 5 — Supra-1.5 and the architecture experiments (late Jul – Aug 2026).**
**Supra-1.5-50M Base/Instruct (EXP)** refreshes the 50M line, holding a **constant BLiMP 67.4** and shipping in three formats — safetensors, **GGUF** (llama.cpp/Ollama/LM Studio/Jan), and **ONNX via `onnx-community`**, which means Supra-1.5 runs **in a browser tab through Transformers.js** (§XIV). Their eval writeup contains a genuinely novel small-model finding: **raw vs. normalized inference splits by task type** — factual/scientific tasks score best on raw text (formatting carries the fact), while math and logic tasks improve under normalization (normalization exposes structure). At 50M parameters, *evaluation formatting is a capability variable*.

Then two architectural swings that have nothing to do with scaling at all:

- **SupraBrain-50M** (~Aug 2) — a **hybrid non-attention** model: **Gated DeltaNet linear recurrence in 3 of every 4 layers** + **sliding-window attention (256) every 4th layer** + one global-attention layer, **surprise-gated updates** (`SurpriseBeta` scales the update by local prediction error), a **digit-splitting byte-level BPE** (vocab 23,808) to fix small-model arithmetic, half-untied heads with rank-32 adapters, and a **Muon + AdamW hybrid optimizer** (Newton-Schulz orthogonalization on 2D matrices, AdamW on everything else) — *the same Muon lineage K3 uses at 2.78T, running on 5B tokens at 50M*. Result: SciQ 70.2%, PIQA 63.0%, LAMBADA 30.7%, wikitext word-ppl 48.8 — a clear jump over the vanilla 50M line.
- **SupraElegans-500k** (~Aug 9) — the most radical: **not a Transformer at all.** 612k parameters arranged as a **sparse, signed, recurrent neural graph** inspired by the *C. elegans* nervous system — sensory / interneuron / output populations, ~10-20 edges per neuron, **no attention, no positional encoding, no KV cache**. Context lives in a **persistent per-neuron membrane potential** updated token by token: `v[t+1] = clamp(leak·v[t] + incoming + bias, -6, 6)`, `a = tanh(v - threshold)`, 3 propagation micro-steps per token. Trained with truncated BPTT; stability required both `1/√(fan-in)` scaling and the `[-6,6]` clamp. Scores are near-chance (PIQA 49.7%, WinoGrande 52.0%) and the card says so honestly — but it is a live, running, downloadable **counterfactual to the Transformer** that exists only because 500k-parameter research is cheap enough to gamble on.

**Phase 6 — Supra2: the generational rewrite (Aug 2026).** — *see the dedicated deep-dive below.*

### Supra2-100M — The Flagship, In Depth

If Supra-50M was the model that made the community notice SLMs, **Supra2** is the one that shows what the field learned in three months. It is not "Supra-50M but bigger." Every layer of the stack — architecture, tokenizer, data mixture, optimizer, LR schedule, SFT recipe — was replaced. The collection is titled, in SupraLabs' own words, *"All Supra2 TOTALLY NEW! Models."* Two models shipped: **Supra2-100M Base** (~Aug 3, 2026) and **Supra2-100M Instruct** (~Aug 4, 2026), and the Instruct model immediately became their most-downloaded release (**1.65k downloads, 34 likes** within days).

#### 1. What changed from Supra-50M → Supra2-100M

| | **Supra-50M** (May 2026) | **Supra2-100M** (Aug 2026) | Why it matters |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Architecture | **Llama** decoder-only | **Qwen3** decoder-only | Adds **QK-Norm**; SupraLabs' May blog argued Llama ≈ Qwen at sub-1M — at 100M the difference finally shows up |
| Parameters | 51.8M | **100.7M total / 75.5M non-embedding** | Non-embedding count is the honest number: 25M is tokenizer overhead |
| Tokenizer | Custom 32,000 BPE, trained on 500k FineWeb-Edu docs | Custom **`supra2-tokenizer`, 32,768** (GEMM-friendly power of two) + ChatML + reserved tokens | Reserved `<|im_start|>`, `<think>`, `<tool_call>`, `<|reserved_0..15|>` so downstream fine-tunes never resize embeddings |
| Hidden / Layers / Heads | 512 / 12 / 8 (GQA 4 KV) | **768 / 12 / 12** (GQA 4 KV, head dim 64) | Wider, not deeper — a direct answer to their own "Depth vs. Width" research question |
| Intermediate (SwiGLU) | 1,408 | **2,048** | |
| Training tokens | 20B (~386 tok/param) | **30B (300 tok/param)** | Both far past Chinchilla's 20 |
| Data | 100% FineWeb-Edu (`sample-100BT`) | **70% FineWeb-Edu (`sample-350BT`) + 30% DCLM-100BT** | First deliberate *mixture* — their "Data-Mix Showdown" experiment, applied |
| LR schedule | **Cosine** decay | **WSD (Warmup–Stable–Decay)** with `1-√` decay to 0 | The modern SLM schedule; makes mid-run checkpoints usable and annealing controllable |
| Optimizer | AdamW fused | AdamW fused (β 0.9/0.95, ε 1e-8), peak LR **1e-3**, wd 0.1, clip 1.0, **router-free z-loss 1e-4** | z-loss stabilizes logit scale — borrowed straight from frontier MoE practice |
| Batch | — | **262,144 tokens/step** (micro 16 × grad-accum 16 × 1,024 seq), **114k steps** | ~30B tokens exactly |
| Precision | bfloat16 | bfloat16, `torch.compile` | |
| Final context | 1,024 | 1,024 trained / 2,048 config max (untested) | Honest labeling of an untested config value |
| Hardware | single GPU | **1× RTX 5090 (32GB)** pretrain, **1× RTX 5060 Ti (16GB)** for the instruct tune | Still one consumer card |
| Data packing | — | Flat `uint16` token stream, contiguous 1,024-chunks, **no padding, no document masking** | They disclose the tradeoff: attention can cross document boundaries |

**The headline architectural change is QK-Norm.** Qwen3 applies RMSNorm to the query and key projections before attention. At 100M parameters trained with a 1e-3 peak learning rate — aggressive for the size — attention logits are the first thing to blow up. QK-Norm is what lets them run that LR on a single card without loss spikes. Combined with **z-loss** (penalizing the log-partition function so output logits don't drift) and the **WSD** schedule, Supra2's training recipe is essentially a scaled-down version of what 2026 frontier labs run — the same stability toolkit K3 uses at 2.78T, applied at 100M.

#### 2. Benchmarks — what 100M actually buys

All numbers via **EleutherAI LM-Eval Harness at standard settings** (SupraLabs confirmed this on the model's discussion page when asked whether results were zero-shot — a small but real mark of methodological transparency):

| Model | Params | PIQA (acc_norm) | HellaSwag (acc_norm) | ARC-Easy (acc_norm) | ARC-Challenge (acc_norm) |
| --- | :-: | :-: | :-: | :-: | :-: |
| OpenAI GPT-2 Small | 124M | 0.62 | 0.31 | 0.39 | 0.22 |
| Supra-50M-Base | 51.8M | 0.62 | 0.32 | 0.46 | 0.25 |
| **Supra2-100M Base** | **100.7M** | **0.65** | **0.36** | **0.48** | **0.25** |
| GPT-X-125M | 125M | 0.65 | 0.37 | 0.51 | 0.25 |
| BananaMind-2-Pro-Preview-EXP | ~? | 0.67 | 0.40 | 0.51 | 0.27 |

**Reading this table honestly** — which is the whole reason SupraLabs is worth documenting:

- **Against GPT-2 Small, Supra2 wins across the board** (+0.03 PIQA, +0.05 HellaSwag, **+0.09 ARC-Easy**, +0.03 ARC-C) at *fewer* parameters (100.7M vs 124M). Seven years of data curation and training-recipe progress, measured cleanly.
- **Against its own predecessor**, doubling 51.8M → 100.7M and 20B → 30B tokens bought **+0.03 PIQA, +0.04 HellaSwag, +0.02 ARC-Easy, and exactly 0.00 on ARC-Challenge.** That is the diminishing-returns curve made visible. Supra-50M's *strength* was BLiMP and SciQ (linguistics and science recall); Supra2's gains are concentrated in **HellaSwag and PIQA — the commonsense/long-context tasks Supra-50M was explicitly weakest on**, and which SupraLabs named as their next target in the Supra-50M release post. They fixed the thing they said they'd fix.
- **They lose to GPT-X-125M on ARC-Easy and to BananaMind-2-Pro on everything**, and they published that table anyway. An org optimizing for optics would have cropped those two rows.
- **ARC-Challenge stuck at 0.25 = pure chance (4-way).** No amount of parameter-doubling at this scale produces multi-step scientific reasoning. This is the SLM ceiling, stated in numbers.

#### 3. The Instruct model — a real chatbot at 100M parameters

**Supra2-100M-Instruct** is a **full fine-tune** (not LoRA) of the Base model on a **16GB RTX 5060 Ti**, using **~300M tokens** of SFT data with a deliberately engineered mixture:

| Source | Share | Purpose |
| --- | :-: | --- |
| `smol-smoltalk` | **77.5%** | Core multi-turn conversational ability |
| Synthetic Basic Arithmetic | **9.3%** | Direct patch for the classic small-model arithmetic hole |
| `qwedsacf/grade-school-math-instructions` | 4.5% | GSM-style word problems |
| `no_robots` | 3.4% | Human-written, high-quality instruction following |
| Style Rewrite of `smol-smoltalk` | 2.5% | Tone/format diversity |
| Style Rewrite of `no_robots` | 1.5% | Tone/format diversity |
| Templated `b-mc2/wikihow_lists` | 1.2% | Structured list/procedural output |

Two things stand out. First, **9.3% of the entire SFT budget is synthetic arithmetic** — a deliberate, targeted intervention against a known failure mode, the same instinct behind SupraBrain's digit-splitting tokenizer. Second, the **"style rewrite" duplicates (4% combined)**: taking existing conversations and re-rendering them in different voices, so the model learns that *content and style are separable* rather than memorizing one register. That's a sophisticated data-augmentation choice for a hobby-scale SFT run.

**The chat interface is standard ChatML**, which means Supra2-Instruct drops into any existing tooling without a custom prompt builder — a real upgrade over Supra-50M-Instruct, which used the Alpaca `### Instruction:` template and silently reverted to base-model behavior if you got the format wrong:

```jinja
{% for m in messages %}<|im_start|>{{ m['role'] }}
{{ m['content'] }}<|im_end|>
{% endfor %}{% if add_generation_prompt %}<|im_start|>assistant
{% endif %}
```

**What it can actually do** (from the 11 published sample conversations): it holds **genuine multi-turn context** — asked "What is AI?", then "Can you give me more information about how it will influence work life?", then "Thank you," it tracks the thread and closes gracefully ("I'm glad I could help"). It produces **structured markdown** with bold headers and bullets. It writes a competent free-verse poem about the sea. It answers "name the top three Microsoft products" with a clean, correct list. It attempts genuine technical explanation on "why does my WiFi keep disconnecting."

**What it cannot do — and this is the more important half.** The published samples are a remarkably honest catalog of 100M-scale failure:

- **Factual collapse.** "The Japanese attack on Pearl Harbor, which occurred on **December 7, 2001**." Einstein "born **July 15, 1879, in Berlin**" (real: March 14, in Ulm), awarded the Nobel "**for his work on the theory of relativity**" (real: the photoelectric effect). Harry Potter "published by **Random House**," Harry called "the **Witch of Hogwart**."
- **Semantic drift on rare tokens.** Asked for fast-food pros and cons, it glides from "pros and cons" into "**contraception**" — a phonetic/embedding-neighborhood slip that a larger model's sharper representations would prevent.
- **Narrative disintegration.** The "little red robot" story starts coherently and dissolves into a loop of "Mama / Maria / Maddy / Mommy / Momma / Mummy / Mango / Muddy Mummy" — the model losing entity identity as context grows.

SupraLabs publishes all of this *in the model card itself*, right next to the good samples, then states the boundary in the Intended Use section: **research, basic chat, education, ablations — explicitly not production, not factual QA, not advice or decision support, English only.** A 100M model that talks fluently and is confidently wrong is the single most useful artifact in the SLM literature for teaching the difference between **fluency and knowledge** — Supra2 has the first at near-full strength and almost none of the second.

#### 4. Distribution — Supra2 runs everywhere

Both models ship in the full 2026 SLM distribution stack: **safetensors** (Transformers), **2 GGUF quantizations** for Base and **3** for Instruct (llama.cpp / Ollama / LM Studio / Jan), plus a live **Hugging Face Space demo** (`Axionlab-official/Supra2-100M-Instruct-Demo`). At ~0.1B in `Q4_K_M`, the whole model is on the order of **60-80MB** — smaller than a phone photo album, runnable on a laptop CPU, and following the Supra-1.5 precedent, ONNX-convertible for browser inference via Transformers.js.

#### 5. What comes next — the announced Supra2 family

The Base card names four unreleased models, and the shape of the list is the interesting part:

- **Supra2-Nano — 800k params**
- **Supra2-Small — ~1.3M params**
- **Supra2-Medium — 25M params**
- **Supra2-IMG — "our SOTA small text-to-image model"**

They are scaling **down** from the flagship, not up. Having established the recipe at 100M, the plan is to push the *same* Qwen3 + WSD + curated-mixture stack down to 800k and see how far it holds — turning Supra2 from a single model into a **controlled scaling ladder where only size varies**. That is exactly Experiment 2 of their published research initiative ("Scaling Law Realities for Tiny Models": fixed data, four sizes, find the point of diminishing returns), now running on the newer architecture. And **Supra2-IMG** would be their first generative-image model, extending the family past text entirely.

#### 6. Why Supra2 matters to this history

**Supra-50M proved a small model could beat an old big one. Supra2 proves the SLM field has a *methodology*.** Every choice in it is traceable to a specific published question: Llama-vs-Qwen (their May architecture post) → Qwen3 at 100M. Depth-vs-width (Experiment B) → wider, same depth. Data-mix showdown (Experiment 1) → 70/30 FineWeb-Edu/DCLM. LR schedules for short runs (Experiment D) → WSD with `1-√` decay. Tokenizer bottleneck (Experiment A) → 32,768 vocab, and the non-embedding parameter count reported separately so you can *see* the bottleneck. Arithmetic weakness → 9.3% synthetic arithmetic in SFT. HellaSwag/PIQA weakness in Supra-50M → the exact benchmarks where Supra2 gains most.

That is a research program, executed on two consumer graphics cards, with the negative results left in. **Supra2-100M is what the small end of the frontier looks like when it grows up** — and it is running the same stability tricks (QK-Norm, z-loss, WSD, curated-mixture pretraining, targeted SFT) as the 2.78T model at the other end of §XXIII's stack.

#### What SupraLabs Actually Achieved

1. **Beat GPT-2 124M with 50M parameters** on BLiMP (+13.3), SciQ (+24.0), and ARC-Easy (+10.2) — trained on one GPU, released Apache 2.0.
2. **Put a 51.8M-parameter model at #1 on Hugging Face trending**, ahead of billion-dollar-lab releases — the clearest signal that the community's attention shifted from "biggest" to "smallest that works."
3. **Mapped the scaling curve end to end by hand**: 1,046 → 289k → 500k → 1M → 2M → 8M → 10M → 30M → 50M → **100M (Supra2)** → 350M → 800M, all public, all reproducible. Nobody else has published a continuous, single-org ladder that wide.
3b. **Shipped a second-generation flagship in under 3 months.** **Supra2-100M** (Aug 2026) rebuilt every layer of the stack on published research — Qwen3 + QK-Norm, 32,768 GEMM-friendly vocab, 70/30 FineWeb-Edu/DCLM mixture, WSD `1-√` schedule, z-loss, 300 tok/param — beat GPT-2 Small on all four reported benchmarks *with fewer parameters*, and fixed exactly the HellaSwag/PIQA weaknesses they had publicly named as Supra-50M's flaw. Its Instruct tune holds real multi-turn ChatML conversation from a **16GB** consumer card, and it is their most-downloaded model.
3c. **Proved the SLM ceiling in the same breath.** Supra2's ARC-Challenge stayed at **0.25 — exact chance** — despite doubling parameters and adding 10B tokens, and the model card publishes hallucinations (Pearl Harbor "December 7, 2001"; Einstein born in Berlin; Nobel "for relativity") next to its good samples. Fluency saturates early at this scale; knowledge does not arrive.
4. **Proved distillation and pruning limits empirically** (DistillSupra 10× compression; MicroSupra as the capability floor) instead of citing them.
5. **Invented the SLM's killer app: routing.** Supra-Router-51M is a working answer to "when do you actually need the trillion-parameter model?" — the piece that makes hybrid edge/cloud AI economically real.
6. **Shipped genuine architecture research at hobby scale** — Gated DeltaNet + sliding-window + surprise gating + Muon (SupraBrain), and a non-Transformer recurrent neural graph (SupraElegans). At 50M-500k params, a *wrong* idea costs an afternoon, so wrong ideas actually get tried. This is the SLM field's structural advantage over frontier labs.
7. **Built the full distribution stack**: safetensors + GGUF (Q2_K_L→BF16) + ONNX, so the same model runs on a server, a terminal (Ollama/llama.cpp/LM Studio/Jan), a browser tab (Transformers.js), and — demonstrably — **a 1 GHz AMD Athlon from 1999 without SSE2, at 1.3 tok/s via llama2.c**. Plus public datasets (Supra Titles Corpus 150K/115K/12K/130K, SupraThink-500x, Prompt-Routing-Dataset) and live demo Spaces.
7b. **Published a real research agenda, not just weights** — the **Open SLM Research Initiative** (May 29): seven controlled ablations on data mixtures, tiny-scale scaling laws, epoch counts, tokenizer bottlenecks, depth-vs-width, context penalties, and LR schedules for short runs, with a commitment to release every checkpoint and unedited log. Supra2 is the first model built on its answers.
8. **Re-democratized AI research**: their headline claims are all footnoted "GTX 750 Ti 4GB," "RTX 5060 Ti 16GB," "one minute of training." The barrier to *doing* language-model research went from a datacenter to a used graphics card.

**The honest limitations** — which SupraLabs states themselves and which any history should record: these models hallucinate freely, score at chance on MMLU, are English-only, have 1-2K context, no safety tuning, and are explicitly "not for production." An SLM is not a small LLM. It is a **component**: a router, a classifier, a titler, a summarizer, a first-draft generator, a research instrument. The SupraLabs catalog is valuable precisely because it never pretends otherwise.

### BDH-CQ — The Other Kind of Small: When 150M Parameters Broke the ARC-AGI Cost Frontier (Aug 2026)

**Paper:** *BDH-CQ: In-Context Learning with Recurrent Latent Reasoning* — Björn Engdahl, Adrian Kosowski, Jan Chorowski, Zuzanna Stamirowska, Przemysław Uznański, Junlin Jiang, Rohan Phadke (Pathway), Remigiusz Kinas (Bielik AI), Richard Zhong (NYU). **arXiv 2608.09888**, submitted Aug 10, 2026; **#1 Paper of the Day** on Hugging Face (Aug 11), 600+ upvotes. Code for the task generators: `github.com/pathwaycom/arc-task-gen`.

If SupraLabs is the SLM movement's *populist* wing — consumer GPUs, GGUF, GPT-2 as the benchmark to beat — **BDH-CQ is its *aristocratic* wing**, and it makes a claim the Supra catalog never could: that a small model can be **state of the art at something the frontier labs care about**. Not state of the art in its weight class. **State of the art, period, on a Pareto axis the big models cannot follow it onto.**

**The headline.** A **150M-parameter** model scores **29.5% pass@2 on the public ARC-AGI-1 evaluation set** (400 tasks) at a computed **$0.00070 per task** — seven hundredths of a cent, measured as ~**0.85 H200 GPU-seconds** at $3/H200-hour. On the ARC Prize Foundation's score-versus-cost plane, **no plotted system reaches that accuracy at equal or lower cost**: it breaks through the previously reported cost–accuracy Pareto frontier. Against the leaderboard's July 2026 numbers it is ~**57× cheaper** than **GPT-5.6 Luna (Low)**, which scores 34.2% at $0.040/task; even after OpenAI's **80% price cut to Luna on July 30, 2026**, BDH-CQ remains ~**11× cheaper** for 4.7 points less accuracy. For comparison, ARC Prize lists **HRM at $1.48/task and TRM at $1.76/task** — roughly **2,100–2,500× BDH-CQ's cost**.

**Why this belongs in the SLM book and not the frontier book.** Every previous entry in §XIII argues that small models are *components* — routers, titlers, classifiers, research instruments — and that **knowledge does not arrive at small scale** (Supra2's ARC-Challenge stuck at exact chance 0.25 is the canonical number). BDH-CQ doesn't refute that. It **sidesteps** it. ARC-AGI is deliberately a benchmark of *skill-acquisition efficiency*, not stored knowledge (Chollet 2019): the grids are small colored rasters requiring almost no factual recall or linguistic fluency, and the rule must be inferred from three or four demonstrations at inference time. BDH-CQ's thesis is that **the thing 150M parameters cannot hold is facts, and the thing they were never the bottleneck for is reasoning** — provided you stop paying for reasoning in tokens.

#### 1. The architecture — post-Transformer provenance

BDH-CQ is built on **BDH ("Baby Dragon Hatchling")**, Pathway's post-Transformer architecture introduced in *The Dragon Hatchling: The Missing Link between the Transformer and Models of the Brain* (Kosowski, Uznański, Chorowski, Stamirowska, Bartoszkiewicz — arXiv 2509.26507, Sep 2025; also a #1 HF Paper of the Day). BDH is a **scale-free graph of locally-interacting neuron particles** with **high-dimensional sparse positive activations (~5% active)**, low-rank communication, **no KV cache**, and working memory carried by **Hebbian synaptic plasticity** rather than a growing context buffer. Its GPU formulation (`BDH-GPU`) is ReLU-low-rank transformations plus **linear attention in a large neuron/feature space**, and it matches **GPT-2-class performance from 10M to 1B parameters** on language and translation at equal data — Transformer-like scaling laws from a non-Transformer computational model, with **monosemanticity and state-level interpretability** as designed-in properties rather than post-hoc probes. It is, in other words, the same genre of bet as **SupraElegans-500k** (§XIII, Aug 9) — *what if not a Transformer?* — except funded, staffed, and carried all the way to a leaderboard.

BDH-CQ extends BDH with two distinct memories, and the separation is the whole idea:

| | Role | Update |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Contextual memory `S_t`** | In-context learning. Demonstrations are ingested **sequentially**, each one updating a recurrent associative state — *not* compressed into a single task vector | `S_t = U_θ(S_{t-1}, D_t)`, θ frozen. Plays attention's role without a growing KV cache; the linear-attention special case is `S_t = S_{t-1} + U_θ(D_t)` |
| **Reasoning workspace `H_r`** | Latent computation for the current query, iterated over model depth | `H_0 = E_θ(x*, S_K)`; `H_{r+1} = F_θ(H_r, S_K)` for r = 0…R−1; `ŷ = G_θ(H_R)` |

**No intermediate state is ever decoded into language, and no parameters are updated at inference time.** Dimensions and exact update rules are, the authors state plainly, **proprietary** — the one place this paper falls short of the SLM movement's open-everything ethic.

#### 2. Why it is cheap — the structural argument

This is the part that matters beyond ARC. A chain-of-thought reasoning model buys extra computation by **emitting tokens**: every intermediate state is projected through a discrete vocabulary, sampled, appended, and re-consumed. Cost and latency grow with the trace, and the trace is *serial*. BDH-CQ buys the same computation by **iterating a continuous state**, which means (a) no vocabulary bottleneck on intermediate representations, (b) no context growth, and (c) **one state can hold several candidate hypotheses in superposition** — the theoretical result the paper leans on is Zhu et al. 2025, showing continuous thoughts can encode and expand **multiple search frontiers in parallel** for graph reachability. Stamirowska's framing: *"Today's AI pays a steep token cost for reasoning, but that cost is imposed by architecture, not by any law of intelligence… The bottleneck was never intelligence. It was design."*

This gives the SLM field a **second economic engine** alongside over-training. Over-training (§XIII opening) buys a permanently cheaper model by moving cost from inference to pretraining. **Latent reasoning buys a permanently cheaper *thought* by moving reasoning out of the token stream entirely.** They compose.

**Reasoning effort is a dial, not a rewrite.** BDH-CQ is trained with *varying* latent-reasoning depth, so effort is selectable at inference:

| Effort | Pass@2 | Cost reduction |
| --- | :-: | :-: |
| **HIGH** | **29.5%** | 0% |
| MEDIUM | 27% | 11% |
| LOW | 21% | 22% |

A separate MIN-effort run on the full evaluation cost **one third** of standard ($0.00088 vs $0.00265 per task on that measurement) for 111/400 instead of 118/400 — a −1.75-point difference the authors explicitly label **statistically unresolved** (McNemar p = 0.167). That is a *latent* analogue of GPT-5's Instant/Thinking router and Claude's adaptive thinking (§XII), running at 150M parameters.

#### 3. What it can and cannot do — the behavioral map

The paper's more valuable half is the ablation program, run on **freshly generated ARC-like tasks created after the model was frozen**, with deterministic oracles — the methodological standard §XIII keeps asking for.

- **Contextual binding is excellent.** Tasks defining a fresh color permutation entirely through demonstrations: **96/96 held-out outputs correct at rank one**, as simultaneous bindings scale from two to eight. Dense, task-specific mappings introduced only through context are recovered and applied consistently.
- **Simple operators extrapolate without a ceiling.** Boundary propagation: **48/48** across distances 2–8 (demonstrations only showed 1–3). Motif copying: **48/48** as target sites go from one to four.
- **Ordering and nesting break — differently.** Ordering is near-saturated to five objects, then collapses: 29/36 at length six, 8/24 at seven, **1/24 at eight**. Nested containment holds to depth four, then 29/36 at depth five. The *signatures* differ: at ordering-length-eight only **3/24** outputs even have correct dimensions (whole-output construction fails), while **all 36** depth-five nesting outputs have correct dimensions with **>99.9% mean cell accuracy** — a single wrong containment decision. Two failures that one aggregate score would have merged.
- **Demonstration coverage is a capability variable.** Re-running byte-identical hard inputs with one demonstration *at the test complexity*: depth-five nesting goes **19/24 → 24/24**; length-eight ordering goes **0/24 → 13/24**. So nesting's cliff is a failure to *extrapolate*, while long ordering retains a genuine **execution bottleneck**. This is a rare clean separation of "can't generalize the rule" from "can't carry out the rule."
- **Composition is representation-dependent, not difficulty-dependent.** Relocation, reflection, and rotation alone: **72/72 each**. Rotation ∘ relocation: **72/72**. Reflection ∘ relocation: **47/72**. Demonstration-defined color swap: **26/72 alone**, and composed with relocation **0/72** — and only in one fixed-layout motif family at that (1/24 in each shuffled family). Some operations simply do not survive being combined.
- **Consistency is the honest weak spot.** On ConceptARC the system hits **77.92% test-pair pass@2 but only 59.38% strict-task pass@2** — an 18.5-point gap. **52 of 160 tasks** have one or two correct test inputs but fail as tasks. Under a rule-induction account of ARC, a correctly induced rule should transfer to *every* test input; partial success is evidence the transformation **is not being applied consistently**. The authors say so in exactly those terms.

| Set / condition | Unit | N | pass@1 | pass@2 [95% Wilson] |
| --- | --- | :-: | :-: | :-: |
| **ARC-AGI-1 public** | tasks | 400 | 97 (24.25%) | **118 (29.50%)** [25.24, 34.15] |
| | test pairs | 419 | 108 (25.78%) | 130 (31.03%) |
| ConceptARC, semantic IDs | tasks | 160 | 73 (45.63%) | 95 (59.38%) [51.63, 66.68] |
| ConceptARC, **opaque IDs** | tasks | 160 | 72 (45.00%) | 96 (60.00%) [52.26, 67.27] |

By ConceptARC family, **FilledNotFilled, TopBottom2D, ExtendToBoundary** top out at 9/10 while **Copy and Order** sit at 2/10 — and the paper immediately notes the Wilson intervals for 9/10 and 2/10 **overlap broadly** (59.6–98.2% vs 5.7–51.0%), so this is *a profile, not a ranking*. That sentence is the kind of statistical self-restraint that should be table stakes and usually isn't.

#### 4. The verification story

For a proprietary-internals paper claiming a leaderboard record, this is unusually well-defended:

- **Independent black-box audit** by co-authors from **Bielik AI** and **NYU**, without weight access, under a documented protocol — **reproduced 29.5% pass@2**.
- **Łukasz Kaiser** — co-author of *Attention Is All You Need* and a Pathway advisor — states he **replicated the ARC-AGI-1 results himself**: *"Pathway shows that model architecture, not just scale, can drive the next leap in AI reasoning."* (Advisor status is a real caveat; the replication is still more than most releases offer.)
- **Confound control:** an opaque-identifier replication replaced semantic `task_id`s with cryptographic labels and mixed concept areas across batches. Aggregate performance **did not move** (374/480 pairs in both conditions; 96/160 vs 95/160 tasks; exactly six semantic-only and six opaque-only successes). The authors then decline to over-claim: it rules out that combined confound, but does **not** make ConceptARC a fresh benchmark, rule out training exposure, or separate the two interventions factorially.
- **Determinism:** repeated identical requests were **byte-identical** at both effort tiers across all 419 test inputs.
- **Anti-inflation note:** in the opaque replication, **all 75 single-candidate records were already correct at rank one** — so "pass@2" here should not be read as two independent attempts on every input.
- **Training-data provenance is disclosed:** ARC-AGI-1 training split, **RE-ARC**, **ConceptARC**, **ARC-Heavy**, **ARC-GEN100K** plus private curation and augmentation. Critically, **no evaluation-task demonstrations and no task identifiers participate in training**, and **nothing is optimized at inference** — which is exactly the line separating BDH-CQ from HRM/TRM, whose ARC pipelines are *transductive* (they run backward passes on augmented evaluation puzzles with learned per-puzzle identity embeddings, which is where their ~$1.50/task comes from).

#### 5. Why it matters to this history

**BDH-CQ is the first result that lets the SLM movement claim a frontier benchmark rather than a weight class.** Read against the rest of §XIII:

1. **It splits "small" into two different bets.** SupraLabs asks *how much of a language model survives compression* — and answers: fluency yes, knowledge no. Pathway asks *how much reasoning you can buy without tokens* — and answers: enough to beat the Pareto frontier, on tasks that don't need knowledge. **The SLM ceiling is a knowledge ceiling, not a reasoning ceiling.** That reframing is the paper's largest contribution to this book.
2. **It validates the non-Transformer gamble at scale.** SupraElegans-500k was a C. elegans-inspired recurrent graph scoring near chance — a legible counterfactual nobody could deploy. BDH is the *same instinct* (local interaction, sparse positive activity, persistent state, no KV cache, Hebbian plasticity) carried to GPT-2-class language performance at 10M–1B **and** a leaderboard record at 150M. §XIII's structural claim — that small scale is where wrong ideas are cheap enough to try — gets its first result that the frontier has to answer.
3. **It gives the routing stack a third tier.** Supra-Router-51M decides *local vs. cloud*. BDH-CQ suggests a middle rung: a class of hard-reasoning, low-knowledge queries that neither a 100M chat SLM nor a 2.78T MoE should handle, because a 150M latent reasoner does them for a fraction of a cent.
4. **It attacks the cost side of the data wall from the other end.** §XXIII argues sparsity is the only road left because dense scaling died at the data wall. BDH-CQ argues that the *inference* cost of reasoning is likewise an architectural choice, not a law — and Pathway reports **Transformer-like pretraining scaling laws from 1B to 600B parameters** with latent reasoning preserved, plus BDH-inherited tensor-sharding patterns they claim make **1T-scale training tractable**. If that holds, "post-Transformer" stops being a §XIII curiosity and becomes a §XXIII contender.

**The honest limitations**, which any history should record next to the headline: the model's **internal dimensions and update rules are proprietary** (unreproducible in a way SupraLabs' catalog is not); ARC-AGI-1 is a **narrow, low-knowledge visual domain** and the model is trained on an ARC-specific mixture — this is not a general reasoner; **29.5% is still under a third of the benchmark**, well below the ~34.2% of the model it undercuts on price and far below human performance; the **18.5-point consistency gap** means it frequently finds an answer without holding a rule; ARC-AGI-2, ARC-AGI-3, mathematical reasoning, and language reasoning are all **stated future work, not results**; and comparison costs are leaderboard-reported **API prices** measured against Pathway's own **hardware-time** computation — a real apples-to-oranges seam the paper flags but cannot close.

Pathway's stated next steps are to scale the model, extend to ARC-AGI-2/3 and math, push into constraint satisfaction (their Sudoku system already does long-horizon latent refinement), and build a **latent-reasoning LLM** that can mix continuous computation with verbalized steps when communication, verification, or tool use requires them. The motivation they cite is Fedorenko & Varley (2016) on language and conceptual reasoning being partly separable systems in humans — the same *"thinking isn't talking"* intuition, now with a leaderboard entry attached.

#### Where SLMs Sit in the 2026 Landscape

The three-track frontier from §XXIV isn't "big vs. small" — it's a **stack**:

```
User request
   ↓
Supra-Router-51M (51.8M, sub-ms, local)   ← decides
   ├── simple/local     → Supra2-100M-Instruct (ChatML, ~70MB at q4) in the browser or Ollama
   ├── hard but *knowledge-free* reasoning
   │                    → BDH-CQ (150M, latent recurrent, ~$0.0007/task, no CoT tokens)
   └── hard/escalate    → Kimi K3 (2.78T MoE, 104B active) / GPT-5.6 Sol / Claude Opus 5
```

Both ends of this stack are running the *same ideas* at 27,000× different scale: RMSNorm, RoPE, GQA, SwiGLU, WSD schedules, the **Muon** optimizer, linear-attention hybrids (Gated DeltaNet in SupraBrain ↔ Kimi Delta Attention in K3), MXFP4/GGUF quantization, distillation from a larger teacher. **The SLM world is not a lesser copy of the frontier — it is the frontier's testbed, and increasingly its front door.**

And the middle rung is the newest and least expected one. **BDH-CQ splits the escalation decision in two**: not every hard query is a *big* query. Requests that need iterated inference but little stored knowledge — puzzles, constraint satisfaction, rule induction, state tracking — can be served by a 150M post-Transformer latent reasoner for a fraction of a cent, while the trillion-parameter tier keeps what it is uniquely for: **knowing things**. That is the sharpest statement §XIII can make about what small models are: **the SLM ceiling is a knowledge ceiling, not a reasoning ceiling** — and BDH-CQ is the proof, holding a state-of-the-art cost–accuracy point on ARC-AGI-1 at 150M parameters while Supra2-100M sits at exact chance on ARC-Challenge. Both facts are true, and they are about different bottlenecks.

## BOOK XIV — THE ACTS: THE WEB AI REVOLUTION: TRANSFORMERS.JS (2023-2026)

### Bringing Frontier Models to the Browser — No Server Required

**Philosophy:** If Transformers (2017) removed the need for recurrence, **Transformers.js** removed the need for a server. Created and led by **Joshua Lochner (@xenovacom / Xenova)** — later joined by core maintainer **Nico Martin (@nico-martin)** and the Hugging Face WebML team — it brings the full 🤗 Transformers ecosystem to JavaScript. Models run **100% locally** in the browser, Node.js, Deno, Bun, and Electron via **ONNX Runtime** (WASM → WebGPU), with no data leaving the device, no hosting costs, and zero Python dependency.

> `npm i @huggingface/transformers` — or `import { pipeline } from "https://cdn.jsdelivr.net/npm/@huggingface/transformers"` — gives you 200+ architectures, 1200+ pre-converted models, and 30+ pipelines directly in the browser.

**How the kernels were optimized — the speedups behind it (as described by Xenova + vLLM/SGLang docs):**

1.  **Execution-graph fusion:** The naive Kimi K3-like graph decomposes into **331 separate GPU nodes** (individual matmuls, convolutions, gating, RMSNorm, SiTU). Each node = a dispatch + memory round-trip. Xenova’s framework (with a **swarm of OpenAI GPT-5.6 Sol agents** — themselves a June 27 2026 frontier model — collaborating for 40+ hours) **automatically discovered fusions** and transformed the graph to **just 22 dispatches per token** — a **~15× reduction in launch overhead**.
2.  **Custom WebGPU kernels for the 3 bottlenecks — exactly K3’s innovations:**
    - **KDA kernel (linear attention with bounded decay):** Fused short convolution (k=4 on q/k/v), low-rank forget gate `f_a/f_b`, full-rank output gate `ssm_g`, and bounded decay `lower_bound*(-5.0)*sigmoid(...)` into one recurrence. Replaces growing KV cache with fixed-size state — like vLLM’s fused KDA decode (convolution + recurrent update + gating + norm in-place).
    - **MLA kernel (Gated Multi-Head Latent Attention):** Fused q-LoRA (rank 1536) + kv-LoRA (rank 512) projections, NoPE handling, and sigmoid output gate. Xenova’s WebGPU version mirrors vLLM’s manual MLA fusion (gate runs parallel to attention, multi-stream decode).
    - **MoE kernel (Stable LatentMoE — 896 experts, top-16):** Fused grouped top-k routing (`e_score_correction_bias`), latent down-proj (7168→3584), expert GEMMs (MXFP4 E2M1 + E8M0 scales), **RMSNorm on the *aggregate* (not per-expert)**, and up-proj 3584→7168 + 2 shared experts (unweighted). Uses Triton/DeepGEMM-style SiTU-GLU `β·tanh(g/β)·σ(g)·β_lin·tanh(u/β_lin)`.
3.  **Correctness-first:** No quantization yet — original bf16/MXFP4 weights kept. **Max logit diff ~1e-7**, i.e., bit-identical behavior. This is the 🤗 **Kernels library** philosophy: fuse without changing numerics.
4.  **Result:** **65 → 406 tok/s on a K3-like model** (≈6.2× speedup) *on WebGPU* — putting a 2.8T-class architecture within browser-adjacent runtimes (Node/Bun/Deno) and proving Xenova’s 2026 thesis that Hugging Face Kernels + WebGPU can bring trillion-parameter open models to local hardware.

#### The *Other* Edge — The Massive “Local CPU” Blind Spot: llama.cpp / GGUF / Ollama (2023) [Audit #3.5]

Your doc makes the edge look like it’s *only* the browser (Transformers.js via WebGPU). In raw downloads/usage, the *bigger* local revolution happened on **CPUs + consumer GPUs** via the terminal:

**llama.cpp (Georgi Gerganov, Mar 10, 2023 — 2 weeks after Meta leaked LLaMA 1 weights):** *Pure C/C++* inference, no Python, no GPU required. `ggml` tensor library + hand-optimized `AVX/AVX2/NEON/Metal/CUDA` kernels that run **LLaMA 7B on a MacBook Air, iPhone, even a Raspberry Pi**. `git clone + make + ./main -m 7B.bin -p "Hello"` → tokens at 5-15 tok/s on CPU. MIT licensed, single binary. Why it matters: made *weight leaks* instantly usable without Nvidia’s stack — the “Napster moment” for open weights.

**GGUF — GPT-Generated Unified Format (Aug 21, 2023, successor to GGML):** The *quantized* single-file format llama.cpp actually runs. Bundles weights + tokenizer + metadata + **K-quant scheme** (`Q4_K_M`, `Q5_K_M`, `Q8_0` — not just naive q4, but per-superblock importance). **Q4_K_M (~3.9 bits/weight)** is the sweet spot: **7B → ~3.8GB, 13B → ~7.3GB, 70B → ~39GB** — fits 4GB-32GB RAM laptops; ~97% of FP16 perplexity thanks to **GPTQ/AWQ-style** accuracy (see PEFT section). Replaced GGML after breaking change; now *the* de facto for local distribution (≈70% of HF community releases, 5,000+ GGUF models on Local AI Zone). `python convert_hf_to_gguf.py → quantize`.

**Ollama (July 2023, “Docker for LLMs”, Go wrapper over llama.cpp):** `ollama run llama3` — the DX that normalized local LLMs. `curl http://localhost:11434/api/generate` (OpenAI-compatible), `Modelfile` (like Dockerfile) for custom prompts/adapters, automatic `Metal` on Mac, `CUDA/ROCm` auto-detect. Default pulls **GGUF Q4_K_M** silently; `ollama pull llama3.1:8b-instruct-q5_K_M` pins variant. Slight 10-20% overhead vs raw llama.cpp due to wrapper, but made `ollama run` the standard dev onboarding (`brew install ollama`). Alongside **LM Studio (GUI, proprietary but polished), Jan, GPT4All**, Ollama turned llama.cpp from hacker tool → mainstream.

**Stack — How users choose (July 2026):**
```
Ollama on any hardware → GGUF (default Q4_K_M)  // beginner / Mac
LM Studio → GGUF Q4_K_M / Q5_K_M               // GUI
NVIDIA GPU, max speed → AWQ 4-bit via vLLM     // production GPU
CPU-only / Pi → GGUF Q4_K_M via llama.cpp       // no GPU
Fine-tune on one GPU → QLoRA (NF4 + LoRA)       // see PEFT
```

## BOOK XVI — THE PARABLES: GAME AI (1990s-2000s)

### Rule-Based and Behavioral AI

**Early Game AI (1990s):** NPC behavior in games like DOOM (1993) used simple rule-based systems—if the player is in range, attack; if health is low, retreat.

**Technique:** Finite State Machines (FSMs)—states with predefined transitions (e.g., patrol, chase, attack, flee).

**The Sims (2000):** Will Wright's game used a sophisticated needs-based AI—each Sim had drives (hunger, social, comfort) and chose actions to satisfy them.

**Key Concept:** Utility-based AI—agents evaluate options based on utility scores and select the highest.

**Black & White (2001):** An experimental game with a learning AI—the creature learned from player reinforcement, associating positive/negative feedback with actions.

**F.E.A.R. (2005):** Used "Goal-Oriented Action Planning" (GOAP)—AI agents planned sequences of actions to achieve goals, creating emergent, tactical behavior.

**Why It Mattered:** GOAP demonstrated that planning (not just reactivity) could make NPCs more intelligent and unpredictable.

**Interactive Narrative AI (2000s):** Façade (2005) and other narrative games used AI to manage story events, character emotions, and player interaction.

**Key Concept:** Belief-desire-intention (BDI) models for character reasoning—characters have beliefs, desires, and intentions that guide their actions.

**Modern Game AI (2020s):** Increasingly uses machine learning—NPCs can learn from player behavior, generate dialogue, and adapt difficulty. See also **§XV’s Roblox AI Girlfriends (2020-2022)** — *Phase 1: pure raw Lua* (no API, hand-coded Markov/dialogue tables, more coherent than Cleverbot) → *Phase 2: lazy API* post-ChatGPT (Nov 2022, `HttpService → OpenAI`); grassroots bridge to LLM NPCs (“Talk to Neko Girl [AI]” Mar 2023, DeterminantAI plugin 2024).

## BOOK XVII — THE TALENTS: THE ECONOMICS & FINANCE OF AI (1966-2026)

### Part A — The State-Funded Age (1966-1984): Governments Pay for the Dream

- **ALPAC Report (1966):** the first *financial* judgment on AI. The US Automatic Language Processing Advisory Committee concluded that machine translation had failed to deliver; government funding was cut. The pattern that would define every AI winter was set: **promise → hype → missed deadline → funding withdrawal.**
- **ARPA/DARPA through the 1960s:** the single largest funder of AI research, guided by J.C.R. Licklider's doctrine of "funding people, not projects" — millions of dollars, few strings, flowing to MIT, Stanford, CMU, and SRI.
- **The Mansfield Amendment (1969):** required DARPA to fund only "mission-oriented" research. Pure, undirected AI no longer qualified. Within a few years, "artificial intelligence" became a toxic phrase in grant proposals — researchers resorted to euphemisms like "informatics" and "knowledge-based systems."
- **The Lighthill Report (1973, UK):** declared AI had failed its promises; the UK's Science Research Council withdrew most AI funding — the official start of the **First AI Winter (1974-1980)**. DARPA cut CMU's Speech Understanding contract (~$3M/yr) in 1974. Universities closed AI labs; a generation of researchers left the field.
- **Japan's Fifth Generation Computer Project (1982):** a ten-year, ~$850M state program to leapfrog the West with AI machines. It terrified the US and Europe into responding: the US founded the Microelectronics and Computer Technology Corporation (MCC, 1982) and launched DARPA's **Strategic Computing Initiative** (1983, ~$1B over a decade).
- **The expert-systems boom — the first real AI revenue:** **XCON** at DEC (John McDermott/CMU, 1978-) configured VAX orders and was estimated to have saved DEC **~$40M over six years**. In the 1980s, corporations spent billions building and maintaining expert systems — the first time AI was a *commercial* line item.
- **The bill came due (1987-1993):** the Lisp-machine makers (Symbolics, Lisp Machines Inc., Thinking Machines) collapsed as general-purpose workstations caught up; corporations wrote off expert-system investments; DARPA's IPTO chief Jack Schwartz cut AI funding "deeply and brutally," dismissing expert systems as "clever programming." The **Second AI Winter** destroyed **billions** — the first time AI losses were borne by *private* capital, not just grants.

**The lesson of Part A:** the funding decides the field. Government money created AI; government withdrawal froze it; commercial money inflated it; and the market's verdict — not the researchers' — ended the boom.

### Part B — The Corporate Laboratory Era (1993-2015): Big Companies Buy the Future

- The statistical turn (§III, §V) needed only *cheap* compute and *plentiful* data — the winters ended because machine learning stopped needing special hardware and ran on commodity PCs. The 1990s dot-com boom, whatever its excesses, built the compute and networking infrastructure AI would later inherit.
- **NVIDIA (founded 1993):** a gaming-graphics company whose chips — via CUDA (2007, §VI) — would become the engine of AI. The most consequential funding decision in AI history was made by **video gamers**.
- **IBM's corporate bets:** Deep Blue beat Kasparov (1997) — marketing triumph; **Watson** won Jeopardy (2011, $1M prize) and IBM created a Watson Group backed by roughly **$1B in investment** (2014) — the first big-corporation "AI bet" of the modern era, before it fizzled into consulting services.
- **The acquisitions that built the modern labs:**
  - **DNNresearch (2013):** Hinton, Krizhevsky and Sutskever's three-person company was auctioned from a Lake Tahoe hotel room — Google won for a reported **~$44M**, outbidding Microsoft, Baidu, and DeepMind. The moment "AI talent" acquired a market price.
  - **DeepMind (2014):** Google acquired the London lab for a reported **$400-650M** after a bidding war — Facebook offered ~$800M, Musk offered Tesla stock; Hassabis chose Google on the condition of an independent London base and a safety board. The first billion-dollar-class AI talent acquisition.
  - **OpenAI (Dec 2015):** Musk, Altman, Brockman, Sutskever and others founded it as a nonprofit with a **$1B pledge**, explicitly to counter Google's dominance — the first "safety-motivated" mega-funding of AI, and the beginning of the modern lab race.
  - **Facebook AI Research (2013, LeCun), Google Brain (2011), Baidu IDL (2013, Ng), Microsoft Research:** every giant built a lab; the researcher talent war began, with salaries and acqui-hires inflating for a decade.

That is why efficiency (FlashAttention, quantization, MoE sparsity — §X, §XXIII) is an *economic* technology, and why token prices keep falling toward marginal hardware cost.

## BOOK XVIII — THE SIGNS: THE SENSES OF AI — MULTIMODAL RESEARCH (2011-2026)

Multimodal AI is the discipline of joining the senses — vision, language, audio, video, and beyond — into models that perceive like animals do: *all at once*. CLIP (§X) is its bridge and ViT (§VIII) its eyes; this section is the rest of the story: how "see + say + hear" went from a research niche to the definition of a frontier model (GPT-4o, Gemini, Kimi K3's MoonViT-V2).

### The Deep Origins (1990s-2015) — Before "Multimodal" Had a Name

- **Audio-visual speech recognition (1990s):** the earliest serious multimodal work — use the *camera* to read lips and the *microphone* to hear (CMU's AVSR, e.g. Bregler & Konig 1994; IBM's "visually steered" ASR). Speech is noisy; vision disambiguates. The template: **one modality disambiguates another.**
- **Multimodal Deep Learning (Ngiam et al., ICML 2011):** the first deep multimodal system — audio + video features learned jointly in an RBM stack, with the founding result that **learning joint representations helps each modality** (shared features beat single-modality features). This paper is the field's origin point.
- **Image captioning (2014-2015):** the task that made "image in, sentence out" mainstream — **NIC (Vinyals et al., 2014: *Show and Tell*, CNN encoder + LSTM decoder)**, **Show, Attend and Tell (Xu et al., ICML 2015)** — the first use of **attention over image regions** in captioning (Bahdanau's mechanism, §VIII, applied to vision), and **Karpathy & Fei-Fei (CVPR 2015)** aligning image regions with sentence fragments (the ancestor of grounding).
- **VQA (Agrawal et al., ICCV 2015):** Visual Question Answering — the task that would define the 2020s: answer a *question about an image*. **MS-COCO** (2014, 330k images with captions from 2015) and **Visual Genome** (2016, dense image-graph annotations) supplied the data.

### The Pretraining Era — Vision-Language BERTs (2019-2021)

If captioning fused CNN+LSTM, the 2019-2021 wave fused *BERT* with images: **ViLBERT (Lu et al., NeurIPS 2019), LXMERT (Tan & Bansal, 2019), VisualBERT (Li et al., 2019), UNITER (Chen et al., CVPR 2020), Oscar (Li et al., ECCV 2020)** — all variants of "run BERT-style masked-language-model pretraining over image-region features + text." They set the pattern: **pretrain on aligned image-text data, fine-tune to VQA/captioning/retrieval.** But they were *heavy* (region features from a separate detector) and *narrow* (task-specific heads).

Then **CLIP (Radford et al., 2021, §X)** changed the paradigm — a single contrastive objective over 400M web image-text pairs produced *zero-shot* classification and a shared embedding space; **ALIGN (Jia et al., 2021)** scaled it with noisy pairs. The bridge existed. The problem became: *how do you attach the bridge to a generative language model?*

### The Frozen-Bridge Era — Connecting Frozen Giants (2022-2023)

The winning pattern (2022-2024): **keep the vision encoder and LLM frozen, train only a small connector** — compute efficiency at foundation-model scale.

- **Flamingo (Alayrac et al., DeepMind, Nov 2022):** the archetype. Frozen vision encoder + frozen Chinchilla LLM, joined by a **Perceiver Resampler** (compress variable images into fixed token counts) and **gated cross-attention layers** inserted between LLM blocks. Result: **few-shot** visual question answering from just a handful of interleaved image-text examples — *without any fine-tuning*. Established gated cross-attention as the standard connector.
- **BLIP / BLIP-2 (Li et al., Salesforce, 2022 → Jan 2023):** **Q-Former** — a tiny learned transformer (32 learnable query tokens) that sits *between* a frozen ViT and a frozen LLM (OPT/FlanT5), trained in two stages (representation, then generation). Only **~188M trainable parameters** — vs Flamingo's ~80B — because the giants stay frozen. *"The compute cheat of the era"*: 2023's LLaVA/Vicuna-style models all descend from this architecture. **InstructBLIP (Dai et al., 2023)** instruction-tuned it.
- **LLaVA (Liu et al., Apr 2023 — *Visual Instruction Tuning*, NeurIPS 2023 oral):** the open-source democratizer. Connect a frozen CLIP ViT to Vicuna with a **single linear projection layer**, and — the key contribution — **synthesize visual instruction data with text-only GPT-4** (images → captions + boxes, GPT-4 → question-answer dialogues; the LLaVA-Instruct-158K dataset). Visual instruction tuning gave a 13B open model GPT-4V-like chat abilities (85.1% relative score on a synthetic benchmark). LLaVA-1.5 (Oct 2023) → LLaVA-NeXT/OneVision (2024) became the community standard — and the *data recipe* (strong LLM generates multimodal training data) is now the universal bootstrap for open MLLMs.
- **The closed-labs giants arrive: GPT-4V (Sep 2023)** — GPT-4 with vision, the first mass-market multimodal frontier; **Gemini 1.0 (Dec 2023)** — *natively* multimodal from day one (trained jointly on image/audio/video/text, not bolted on); **Qwen-VL (Sep 2023)** and the open-VLM wave (InternVL, MiniCPM-V, MobileVLM, Moondream 2024 — VLMs that run on phones and browsers, §XIV).

### The Omni Era — One Model, All Senses (2023-2026)

- **ImageBind (Meta, May 2023 — *One Embedding Space to Bind Them All*):** CLIP extended from 2 to **6 modalities** — images/video, text, audio, depth, thermal, IMU — learned contrastively using *image as the binding hub* (pairs only need to touch images). Open-sourced; demonstrated emergent zero-shot cross-modal retrieval (image → audio, text → depth). The first serious "everything aligned to everything" space.
- **Any-to-any generation: NExT-GPT (2023)** and **CoDi (2023)** — one system that accepts *and generates* text, image, video, audio; **Meta's Chameleon (2024)** — **early fusion**: images and text as *one token stream in one vocabulary* (no connector, the strongest form of unification); **GPT-4o (May 13, 2024)** — *"omni"*: one model natively handling text, image, audio, and *real-time spoken dialogue* at ~320ms latency (the product that made multimodal feel like talking to a person); **Gemini 1.5 (Feb 2024, 1M-token multimodal context)** → 2.0 (Dec 2024) → 2.5 (2025, thinking); **Qwen2-VL/2.5-VL** (2024-2025) and **Qwen2.5-Omni** (Mar 2025) — the strongest open omni models; **Kimi K3's MoonViT-V2** (§XII) — image/video shared encoder trained from scratch, pixel-shuffle 4× token compression; **GPT-5.6** (2026) — unified native multimodal + tool use.
- **Speech & audio language models:** the audio branch — **DeepSpeech** (2014), **Listen-Attend-Spell** (2015), **WaveNet** (2016), **Tacotron** (2017), and **Whisper** (OpenAI, Sep 2022 — 680k hours of multilingual audio, 100+ languages, near-human ASR + translation, MIT license — *the* open ASR of the era); then audio *language* models — **AudioLM/MusicLM** (Google 2023), **Qwen-Audio** (2023), **AudioPaLM** (2023, speech+text unified), TTS at human quality (**VALL-E** 2023, ElevenLabs), and music generation (Suno 2024, Udio) — all the same "tokenize the modality, model it with a transformer" pattern. GPT-4o (2024) merged speech *into* the main model; GPT-Live-1 (Jul 2026, §XV) made it full-duplex.
- **Video LLMs & video generation:** **Video-LLaMA** (2023), **Video-LLaVA** (2023), Qwen2-VL's video branch — LLMs that watch; and the generative side — **VideoPoet** (Google, 2023), **Sora** (OpenAI, Feb 2024, DiT + diffusion over space-time patches — the "world simulator" claim; Sora 2 Sep 2025 in §XV), **Runway Gen-3** (2024), and **Genie** (DeepMind, 2024 — generative *interactive environments* trained purely from video, a step toward world models §XI).
- **Evaluation caught up: MMMU** (Yue et al., Nov 2023 — 57 college-level subjects, ~11.5k multimodal questions; *the* hard multimodal benchmark), **MMBench** (2023), **SEED-Bench** (2023, video+image), **MathVista** (2023, multimodal math), **Video-MME** (2024) — and the field learned that **perception is near-solved but multimodal *reasoning* is not**: GPT-4V-class models ace object recognition and fail college physics diagrams.

### The Unification Thesis

Four architectures have competed for "how do you join the senses":
1. **Connector fusion** (Flamingo/BLIP-2/LLaVA — frozen giants + learned bridge; the 2022-2024 default, cheap and effective),
2. **Early fusion / unified token space** (Chameleon, GPT-4o, K3's MoonViT-V2 — every modality becomes tokens in one vocabulary; the 2025-2026 frontier default),
3. **Embedding hub** (CLIP/ImageBind — alignment without generation; the retrieval/grounding layer),
4. **World-model fusion** (V-JEPA, Sora/Genie — predict the *world*, not tokens; the §XI counter-narrative).

The end state, visible in 2026: **one model, one token vocabulary, all senses** — with the "modalities" differing only in how signals become tokens. That is why GPT-4o, Gemini, and Kimi K3 all converge on the same shape, and why the browser/edge ecosystem (§XIV) now ships VLMs as default. The last open frontier is not *seeing* — it's *understanding what's seen*, which is a reasoning problem (§XI), not a perception problem.

## BOOK XIX — THE BOOK OF LIFE: THE LIVING MACHINE — GENETIC & BIO AI RESEARCH (1950s-2026)

Two distinct research strands share this name, and both run deeper than most histories record: **(1) evolutionary computation** — algorithms inspired by Darwinian selection, one of AI's oldest branches; and **(2) AI for biology** — machine learning applied to genomes, proteins, cells, and drugs, which produced two of the most consequential AI results of the 2020s (AlphaFold, Evo 2). They are converging: 2024-2026 models treat *evolution itself* as a language, and treat *genomes/proteins as text* for LLMs.

### Part A — Evolutionary Computation: AI by Natural Selection (1950s-2026)

**The founding idea (1950s-1970s):** imitate Darwin — keep a *population* of candidate solutions, mutate/recombine them, keep the fittest, repeat.
- **Evolution strategies (Rechenberg, 1964; Schwefel):** mutate real-valued vectors, keep the best (the (1+1)-ES). **Evolutionary programming (Fogel, 1966):** evolve finite-state machines. **Genetic algorithms (John Holland, 1975 — *Adaptation in Natural and Artificial Systems*):** binary-string solutions, **selection + crossover + mutation**, powered by the **schema theorem** (why building blocks survive). GA is the term everyone knows, and Holland's book is the field's bible.
- **Genetic programming (Koza, 1992):** evolve *programs* (Lisp trees) that compute — the 1990s "let evolution write the code" moment. **CMA-ES (Hansen & Ostermeier, 1996-2001):** covariance-matrix-adaptation evolution strategy — arguably the *best* general black-box optimizer ever built; still the default baseline for non-differentiable search.
- **Metaheuristics family:** **simulated annealing** (Kirkpatrick et al., 1983 — physics-inspired, but grouped here), **ant colony optimization** (Dorigo, 1992), **particle swarm** (Kennedy & Eberhart, 1995), tabu search — the "swarm intelligence" toolbox that solved scheduling/routing problems for decades.
- **Neuroevolution:** evolve *network weights/architectures* — **NEAT** (Stanley & Miikkulainen, 2002, evolves topology + weights), **HyperNEAT** (2009, indirect encoding via a "compositional pattern producing network"), **novelty search** (Lehman & Stanley, 2008 — reward *novelty* instead of fitness; surprising state-of-the-art results on deceptive tasks).
- **Evolution meets deep RL (2017):** **OpenAI's *Evolution Strategies as a Scalable Alternative to Reinforcement Learning*** (Salimans et al., 2017) — black-box ES solved MuJoCo tasks competitively with Q-learning, trivially parallelizable across CPUs (no backprop, no value function). A reminder that gradient-free search never died — it waited.
- **The 2023-2026 renaissance — evolution + LLMs:**
  - **FunSearch (DeepMind, Nature Dec 2023):** *"searching in the function space"* — an LLM proposes program variants, an evaluator scores them, the fittest survive, repeat. Result: **the first discoveries on open mathematical problems by an LLM** — new large **cap sets** beyond the best-known (the largest improvement to the asymptotic bound in 20 years) and better online bin-packing heuristics.
  - **AlphaEvolve (DeepMind, May 2025):** FunSearch generalized — a **Gemini-powered evolutionary coding agent** that evolves entire code files across any language, with parallel GPU evaluation. It produced a more efficient **data-center job-scheduling algorithm at Google** and new tensor-decomposition algorithms. Evolution's 70-year arc completes: *hand-coded → evolved → LLM-directed evolution*.

**Where evolution sits in the stack:** it is the universal optimizer when gradients don't exist (programs, architectures, heuristics, molecules) — and with LLMs as the mutation operator, it became a *discovery engine* for 2024-2026 science. It also underpins the biological view of intelligence (see Part B's convergence).

### Part B — AI for Biology: Reading and Writing the Code of Life (1994-2026)

**Foundations:** **DNA computing (Adleman, 1994)** — Leonard Adleman solved a 7-node Hamiltonian-path problem *using actual DNA molecules in a test tube*: computation as chemistry, the strangest proof that biology computes. Bioinformatics matured in parallel: **BLAST** (1990) sequence search, the **Human Genome Project** (2003, ~3 billion base pairs sequenced).

**Protein structure prediction — the 50-year problem that fell (2018-2020):**
- **AlphaFold 1 (DeepMind, Dec 2018, CASP13)** — first deep-learning system to win CASP by a wide margin.
- **AlphaFold 2 (DeepMind, Nov 30 2020, CASP14)** — the watershed: an attention-based network (evoformer blocks doing pairwise **MSA + residue-pair** processing, structure module, iterative refinement) predicted protein structures to **near-experimental accuracy** (median GDT ~92 vs experimental ~90+; CASP14's organizers: *"the protein folding problem is largely solved"*). **Nature (Jul 2021)**; the **AlphaFold Protein Structure Database** (with EMBL-EBI) grew to **200M+ predicted structures** — most of biology's known proteins — used by 2M+ researchers. **Nobel Prize in Chemistry 2024: half to Demis Hassabis & John Jumper (AlphaFold 2), half to David Baker (protein design).**
- **The open ecosystem: RoseTTAFold** (Baker lab, 2021), **ESMFold** (Meta, 2022 — 15× faster, from ESM-2 embeddings), **OpenFold** (2022, open AlphaFold-2 reimplementation), **AlphaFold 3** (May 2024) — extends to *complexes*: proteins + DNA + RNA + ligands + ions, with a diffusion module.

**Protein language models — proteins as text (2019-2024):**
- **ESM family (Rives et al., Facebook/Meta, 2019-2022):** *ESM-1b* (2019) showed masked-protein-language modeling learns **evolutionary structure** (attention over residues ≈ contacts); **ESM-2 + ESMFold** (2022) — a 15B-parameter protein LM that folds 15× faster than AlphaFold 2; **ProGen** (Salesforce, 2020-2022) — generative protein sequences; **ProtTrans** — protein transformers at scale. The insight: **amino-acid sequences are a language, and evolution is the corpus** — millions of species are the "books" it was trained on.
- **ESM3 (EvolutionaryScale, Jun 2024) — simulating evolution:** a 98B-parameter generative model that reasons over **sequence + structure + function simultaneously**, trained on **2.78B proteins** (~1 trillion teraflops, the most compute ever spent on biology at the time). Prompted to generate a fluorescent protein, it produced **esmGFP** — a functional green fluorescent protein only **58% identical** to its nearest natural relative (a jellyfish/coral GFP), a gap ESM3 estimates would take **~500 million years** of natural evolution to cross. *Biology, generated.*
- **Protein design (the other Nobel half):** David Baker's lab, after 20 years of Rosetta, went deep-learning: **ProteinMPNN** (Dauparas et al., 2022) — inverse folding (structure → sequence) with near-perfect design success rates; **RFdiffusion** (Watson et al., Nature 2023) — a **diffusion model over protein backbones** that designs entirely new proteins with specified functions (binders, enzymes, vaccines); **AlphaProteo** (DeepMind, Sep 2024) — de novo **protein binders** with 3-300× better affinity than prior designs, including the first computational binder for VEGF-A (a cancer target that had resisted design). 2026 state of play: design-a-protein → predict-it → synthesize-it is a routine pipeline.

**Genomics language models — DNA as text (2015-2026):**
- The pre-LLM wave: **DeepSEA** (2015, regulatory genomics), **DeepBind** (2015), **DeepVariant** (Google, 2017 — deep-learning variant calling, now standard in clinical genomics).
- The LM wave: **Enformer** (DeepMind, Nature Methods 2021 — transformer predicting gene expression from 200kb of DNA); **DNABERT** (2021); **Nucleotide Transformer** (InstaDeep/NVIDIA, 2022, 2.5B params, 3.2k genomes); **AlphaMissense** (DeepMind, Science Sep 2023 — predicted the effect of **all ~71M possible human missense variants**, classifying ~32% pathogenic vs the ~0.1% clinically known); **scGPT** (2024) — foundation model for single-cell transcriptomics.
- **Evo (Arc Institute + NVIDIA + Stanford, May 2024, Nature Nov 2024):** a 7B-parameter genomic LM trained on **300B nucleotides** of prokaryotic genomes at single-nucleotide resolution — it generates DNA, predicts variant effects, and **designed functional CRISPR-Cas systems** (Cas9/13 variants) end-to-end. *Genomes were already a language — Evo proved you could write them.*
- **Evo 2 (Arc Institute, Feb 19 2025) — the largest AI model in biology:** **40B parameters** trained on **9.3 trillion nucleotides from 128,000+ genomes across all three domains of life** (bacteria, archaea, eukaryotes — including humans and plants), **1M-nucleotide context** (a yeast chromosome), **StripedHyena 2** architecture (convolution + attention hybrid, 3× faster than a Transformer, trained on 2,000+ H100s), open weights + OpenGenome2 dataset. Results: >90% accuracy predicting BRCA1 mutation effects, outperforming on noncoding variants, and **annotating the woolly mammoth genome from sequence alone** (a species it never trained on). The "operating system for biology" claim is now literal: 2026 genomics is a *language-model* field.

**Drug discovery, materials, and medicine:**
- **Molecules:** generative chemistry — **JT-VAE** (2018), **MolGAN**, **REINVENT**, diffusion for molecules (2023); **Insilico Medicine** (2019) used GANs to design a JAK3 inhibitor in 21 days; **Exscientia's DSP-1181** (2020) — the first AI-designed drug to enter human clinical trials. AlphaFold/ESM now front-load the drug pipeline with target structures.
- **Materials:** **GNoME** (DeepMind, Nature Nov 2023) — graph networks discovered **2.2M new crystals, 381,000 predicted stable**, ~45 days of compute instead of centuries of lab work — the materials-science analogue of AlphaFold.
- **Medical imaging (§VIII):** CheXNet (2017) and the FDA-cleared radiology/pathology AI era; MedSAM.
- **Brains & machines:** neuromorphic chips — **IBM TrueNorth** (2014), **Intel Loihi** (2017) — spiking neural networks (SNNs) that mimic biological spikes; **connectomics** — the complete adult **fruit-fly brain connectome** (~140k neurons, ~50M synapses) mapped by Janelia/Harvard/Google (2023-2024), with human-scale connectomics on the horizon; **Blue Brain** (Markram, 2005) and the **Human Brain Project** (2013); **BCIs** — Neuralink (founded 2016; first human implant Jan 2024) and speech-decoding BCIs (2023-2024, decoding imagined speech from cortex at usable word rates); **digital organisms** — Tierra (Ray, 1991), **Avida** (Adami, 1993) — artificial life under real Darwinian selection; **OpenWorm** — a C. elegans digital twin; **genetic circuit design** — Cello (2016), and CRISPR engineering (OpenCRISPR, Evo-designed Cas variants).

### The Convergence: Evolution Is a Language, Biology Is Programmable

The 2020s' deep discovery: **the tools of AI language modeling are the tools of biology.** Amino acids are tokens with a 3.2B-year training corpus; nucleotides are tokens; evolution is the training procedure; and "fitness" is the reward. ESM3's esmGFP, Evo 2's genome writing, and RFdiffusion's protein design are all the same operation: **condition a learned model of life's code on a desired function, then sample.** AlphaFold and Evo 2 sit alongside GPT-5.6 and Kimi K3 (§XII) as the largest models ever trained — the difference is only what language they model. The 2026 frontier: **designer proteins for drugs/carbon capture, genome-scale engineering, digital twins of organisms, and AI-designed clinical trials** — with evolutionary computation (Part A) now the search engine on top (FunSearch/AlphaEvolve for the *algorithms*, protein/genome LMs for the *substrate*).

## BOOK XX — THE CREEDS: THE CORE PARADIGMS

### Understanding the Evolution of AI

**Symbolic AI (1950s-1980s):** Intelligence through explicit rules and logic. ELIZA, A.L.I.C.E., early expert systems. Limitation: brittle, can't handle uncertainty.

**Connectionism (1980s-present):** Intelligence through neural networks. Perceptron, backpropagation, deep learning. Strength: learns from data, generalizes. Limitation: black box, needs massive data.

**Statistical AI (1990s-2010s):** Intelligence through probabilities and data. Markov chains, n-grams, graphical models. Strength: handles uncertainty, transparent. Limitation: hand-engineered features, shallow.

**Deep Learning (2012-present):** Multi-layer neural networks learning hierarchical features. AlexNet, Transformers, GPT. Strength: end-to-end learning, scalable. Limitation: compute-intensive, data-hungry.

**Reinforcement Learning (2010s-present):** Learning through trial and error. AlphaGo, reasoning models. Strength: can learn optimal strategies. Limitation: reward design is challenging.

**Agentic AI (2024-present):** Models as agents that use tools, reason, and act in the world. Kimi K2.5, Agent Swarm. Strength: general-purpose problem solving. Limitation: safety and alignment challenges.

**Energy-Based Models — EBMs (1982-present, Hopfield → LeCun 2006 → Diffusion/JEPA):** Learn energy `E_θ(x,y)` low for correct pairs, high otherwise. Strength: unified view of Hopfield/Boltzmann/RBM/diffusion score/JEPA. Limitation: sampling expensive (pre-diffusion).

**Generative Visual AI — VAEs (2013) / GANs (2014) / Diffusion (2015/2020):** VAEs: stable, probabilistic, blurry. GANs: sharp, fast (1 step), mode collapse. Diffusion: iterative denoising, best quality/diversity, slow (50-250 steps), dominates Sora/Stable Diffusion. Strength: creative synthesis. Limitation: compute vs quality tradeoff.

**Retrieval-Augmented Generation — RAG (2020):** Parametric (LLM) + non-parametric (vector index) memory. Strength: factual grounding, hot-swap knowledge, citations. Limitation: retrieval quality bounds generation.

**World Models — JEPA / Neuro-Symbolic (2022-present):** Predict representations, not pixels/tokens (I-JEPA, V-JEPA 2). Strength: physical reasoning, sample efficiency, planning. Limitation: hard to generate detailed outputs; still research-stage vs scaled LLMs.

**Hardware-Software Co-Design (2007-present):** CUDA (2007), cuDNN/Tensor Cores, TensorFlow (2015), PyTorch (2016), TPUs (2016). Strength: makes depth & scale possible. Limitation: CUDA lock-in, cost.

**Classic RL — From DQN to AlphaZero (2013-2017):** DQN (Atari from pixels) → AlphaGo (MCTS + policy/value) → AlphaZero (self-play zero human). Strength: proves RL can discover superhuman strategy without data. Limitation: needs simulator/perfect rules.

**Efficiency Architecture (2022-2023):** FlashAttention (IO-aware tiling, exact, 2-4×, O(N) memory) → Mamba (selective SSM, linear O(N)) → Ring Attention (blockwise across devices, enables 1M). Strength: makes 1M context trainable/inferable. Limitation: engineering complexity.

**Vision-Language (ViT 2021 + CLIP 2021):** ViT: pure Transformer beats CNNs; CLIP: contrastive 400M image-text bridge. Strength: zero-shot transfer, unlocks DALL-E/Stable Diffusion/GPT-4V/K3 MoonViT. Limitation: needs web-scale noisy pairs.

**PEFT & Quantization (2021-2023):** LoRA (low-rank adapter, 0.1% params), GPTQ/AWQ (lossless 4-bit via Hessian/saliency), QLoRA (NF4 + LoRA = 65B on one GPU). Strength: democratizes fine-tuning & inference (7B→4GB). Limitation: adapter merging, slight quality loss.

**Prompting & Instruction Tuning (2022-2023):** InstructGPT (SFT+RM+PPO) → CoT (“let’s think step by step”, +40% GSM8K) → ToT (search over thought trees). Strength: unlocks reasoning without weight updates; recipe for ChatGPT. Limitation: brittle, prompt-sensitive.

**Local CPU Inference (2023-present):** llama.cpp (C++ on MacBook/Pi) + GGUF (Q4_K_M quantized single-file) + Ollama (“Docker for LLMs”, `ollama run llama3`). Strength: private, offline, no GPU. Limitation: slower than data-center GPUs (5-15 tok/s CPU).

## BOOK XXIII — THE NEW CREATION: WHY KIMI K3 CHANGES EVERYTHING — THE MoE ENDGAME

### Conditional Computation Becomes the Scaling Architecture

Everything in §XII describes *what* Kimi K3 is. This book explains the lineage and meaning of its sparsity. The crucial origin is not a 2026 model but Noam Shazeer et al.'s 2017 paper, **“Outrageously Large Neural Networks: The Sparsely-Gated Mixture-of-Experts Layer”** ([arXiv:1701.06538](https://arxiv.org/abs/1701.06538)). That work turned a decades-old idea—let different specialists handle different inputs—into a practical, trainable, distributed neural-network layer.

Its achievement should be stated precisely. Mixture-of-Experts does **not** make parameters free, and a sparse model is not equivalent to a dense model with the same total parameter count. MoE instead changes the relationship between three quantities that dense networks tie together:

1. **Total parameters:** the model's full bank of learned weights.
2. **Active parameters:** the subset used for one token or example.
3. **FLOPs and communication:** the actual execution cost of routing and evaluating that subset.

MoE makes the first quantity grow much faster than the second. That is the real breakthrough: **more learned capacity per unit of arithmetic**, purchased with memory, routing, communication, and optimization complexity.

#### 1. The prehistory: experts existed before sparse expert layers

The original **Mixture of Experts** work (Jacobs et al., 1991; Jordan & Jacobs, 1994) trained several expert networks and a gate that weighted their outputs. Earlier systems often behaved like learned ensembles: the gate decided which model should handle an example, sometimes hierarchically. But if every expert was evaluated and then blended, computation still grew with the number of experts.

The broader idea of **conditional computation** was therefore more ambitious: activate only a small part of a large network for each input. In theory, this could increase parameter count without proportionally increasing FLOPs. In practice, early attempts ran into four linked problems:

- hard or discrete routing is difficult to train by gradient descent;
- experts can collapse, with the gate sending nearly everything to a favored few;
- sparse routing creates tiny per-expert batches, wasting GPU throughput;
- distributing experts creates heavy device-to-device communication.

Shazeer et al. did not invent specialization, but they supplied the algorithmic and systems recipe that made **massively sparse, jointly trained expert layers** work at unprecedented scale.

#### 2. The layer: route first, compute only the winners

Let there be `n` expert networks `E₁ … Eₙ` and a trainable gate `G`. For input `x`, the MoE output is

`y = Σᵢ G(x)ᵢ Eᵢ(x)`.

The decisive condition is sparsity: most components of `G(x)` are exactly zero. If `G(x)ᵢ = 0`, expert `Eᵢ` need not run. A model may therefore store hundreds or thousands of experts while evaluating only `k` of them for a token.

The 2017 paper introduced **Noisy Top-k Gating**. In simplified form:

`Hᵢ(x) = (xW_g)ᵢ + εᵢ · softplus((xW_noise)ᵢ)`

`G(x) = softmax(KeepTopK(H(x), k))`,

where `εᵢ ~ N(0,1)` and every non-top-`k` logit is replaced by `−∞` before softmax. The gate therefore produces a weighted combination of only the selected experts.

Each component has a purpose:

- **learned router logits** decide which experts fit the input;
- **top-k selection** creates conditional computation;
- **softmax over the winners** gives differentiable mixture weights for selected experts;
- **training noise** encourages exploration and supports a smooth estimate of expert load.

With `k > 1`, gradients flow through the selected gate weights as well as through the chosen experts. Modern systems often use top-1 or top-2 routing and may alter the noise, capacity, or balancing method, but the basic pattern—**score, select, dispatch, compute, combine**—comes directly from this formulation.

#### 3. Why the router is the hard part

A router can fall into a self-reinforcing failure mode. Suppose one expert is selected slightly more often early in training. It receives more gradient updates, improves faster, and becomes still more attractive to the router. Soon a few experts receive most tokens while others remain poorly trained or unused. This is called **expert collapse** or load imbalance.

Shazeer et al. addressed it with two auxiliary objectives.

First, expert **importance** for a batch `X` is the sum of its gate weights:

`Importance(X) = Σₓ∈X G(x)`.

The importance loss penalizes the squared coefficient of variation:

`L_importance = w_importance · CV(Importance(X))²`.

Second, the paper defines a differentiable estimate of each expert's **load**—the expected number of examples for which its noisy score enters the top `k`—and penalizes imbalance similarly:

`L_load = w_load · CV(Load(X))²`.

These terms are not cosmetic. In the paper's ablation, training without either balancing loss produced a maximum expert load **17.8 times the mean** and worse perplexity. With both losses, that ratio fell to roughly **1.14** while quality improved. The lesson survived every later MoE generation: **a sparse model is only as real as its routing and utilization.** A trillion nominal parameters do little if the gate trains only a small fraction of them.

Balanced traffic, however, does not prove meaningful specialization. It proves that experts receive work. Specialization must be observed empirically. In the 2017 translation model, experts did develop recognizable syntactic and semantic preferences—for example, contexts involving research and innovation, leadership roles, or rapid change—but such patterns are emergent tendencies, not guaranteed human-readable modules.

#### 4. The systems insight: sparse arithmetic can still be slow

The paper's most durable contribution may be its recognition that theoretical FLOPs are not enough. GPUs prefer large dense matrix multiplications; they are poor at irregular branching and tiny jobs.

If a batch contains `b` items, the router selects `k` of `n` experts, and assignments are balanced, each expert receives only about

`k·b / n`

items. As `n` grows, this **shrinking-batch problem** can make every expert invocation inefficient. The solution was to combine data and model parallelism:

- ordinary layers are replicated across devices as in data parallelism;
- experts are sharded across devices as in model parallelism;
- token assignments from all replicas are gathered into larger per-expert batches;
- expert outputs are returned to their original token positions.

Across `d` devices, the expected expert batch grows to roughly `k·b·d / n`. This is the ancestor of modern **expert parallelism** and its all-to-all token dispatch.

The paper also made experts computationally substantial—feed-forward networks with large hidden layers—so arithmetic per dispatched byte was high enough to amortize network transfer. This remains the central hardware tension of MoE: sparse activation saves matrix multiplications, but routing incurs sorting, packing, all-to-all communication, synchronization, padding, and possible token dropping. An MoE with fewer nominal FLOPs can be slower than a dense model if its kernels or interconnect are poor.

This is why modern MoE progress is inseparable from systems engineering: fused dispatch kernels, capacity-aware routing, topology-aware expert placement, high-bandwidth interconnects, quantization, and overlap of communication with computation.

#### 5. What the 2017 experiments actually proved

The results were startling for their time:

- On the **1 Billion Word** benchmark, a 4.3B-parameter MoE used about **8.9M operations per timestep**, versus **151M** for the best published dense LSTM baseline, yet slightly improved 10-epoch perplexity (34.1 vs. 34.7). At a similar compute budget to the dense baseline, a 4.37B MoE reached **28.0** perplexity versus **34.7** after 10 epochs.
- On a **100B-word** news corpus, the authors scaled to as many as **137B parameters**. The best point was around **68.9B parameters / 65,536 experts**, with 28.9 perplexity versus 47.0 for a compute-matched LSTM baseline—a **39% reduction**. The largest 137.7B model was slightly worse, demonstrating that more experts are not automatically better.
- A translation model with **2,048 experts and 8.7B total parameters** improved WMT'14 English→French and English→German BLEU while using fewer reported operations per timestep than GNMT.
- The paper reported models with more than **99.99% sparsity inside the expert layer** while retaining respectable GPU utilization.

These were LSTM-era models, not Transformers. The MoE layer sat between recurrent layers and was applied at every text position. But the conceptual bridge to modern Transformers was direct: replace some dense Transformer feed-forward blocks with sparse expert feed-forward blocks while leaving attention and other shared components dense. Later systems—GShard, Switch Transformer, GLaM, Mixtral, DeepSeekMoE, and many others—changed the router, expert granularity, capacity policy, and training stack, but retained the same core contract.

#### 6. How to read MoE parameter counts honestly

For a dense network, “parameters” and “parameters used per token” are nearly the same. For MoE they are not. Every serious description should report at least:

- **total parameters**;
- **active parameters per token**;
- **number of routed experts and experts selected**;
- **shared parameters outside the routed blocks**;
- **training/inference FLOPs per token**;
- **memory footprint and precision**;
- **routing balance, capacity policy, and communication setup**.

Applied to the figures stated for Kimi K3 in this document:

- `896 / 16 = 56×` is the **expert-selection ratio**: one token selects 16 of 896 routed experts.
- `2.78T / 104.2B ≈ 26.7×` is the **whole-model total-to-active parameter ratio**.
- `104.2B / 2.78T ≈ 3.75%` is the fraction of total parameters active for one token.

These numbers differ because shared attention, embeddings, routers, norms, and other non-expert weights may execute for every token. Calling all three “56× sparsity” conflates expert routing with whole-model activation.

Nor is there a universal formula that converts an MoE into a “dense-equivalent” parameter count. The geometric mean of active and total parameters is sometimes used as an informal intuition, but it is **not a law** and should not be presented as one. Quality depends on training tokens, routing, expert size, shared depth, optimization, data mixture, communication constraints, and downstream task. The honest comparison is empirical: match training compute, inference compute, memory, latency, and evaluation quality.

#### 7. Does MoE “fake” its parameters?

The useful answer is neither “yes” nor “no.” It depends on which claim is being made.

**The total count is real:** the expert weights are stored, trained, checkpointed, and can be selected by the router. Across many tokens, different experts contribute. They are not zeros or decorative padding.

**The dense comparison is not real:** a 2.78T-total MoE does not perform the arithmetic of a 2.78T dense model on every token. Its per-token transformation is constrained by the active route plus the shared trunk. Total parameters describe the available conditional bank, not the amount of computation applied to one prediction.

**The memory bill remains real:** sparsity primarily reduces compute, not storage. At an idealized 4 bits per weight, 2.78T weights alone require about **1.39 TB**, before scales, metadata, caches, runtime buffers, or sharding overhead. “Open weights” does not mean “runs on an ordinary personal computer.” Quantization makes deployment less impossible; it does not erase the model.

The fairest summary is:

> **MoE does not fake parameters; it makes parameter use conditional. The headline becomes misleading only when total parameters are used as if they measured dense per-token compute or guaranteed capability.**

#### 8. What sparsity buys—and what it does not

**What it buys:**

1. **Capacity per FLOP.** More expert weights can be learned without evaluating every weight for every token.
2. **Potential specialization.** Different routes can model different languages, domains, token types, or computational patterns.
3. **Scalable sharding.** Experts provide natural units for distribution across devices.
4. **Cheaper generation than a same-total-size dense model.** This matters enormously for synthetic data, distillation, and reinforcement-learning rollouts.
5. **A new scaling axis.** Designers can vary total experts, selected experts, expert width, shared depth, and routing granularity separately.

**What it does not buy automatically:**

1. **Free data.** Each expert sees only the tokens routed to it. Increasing the number of experts can reduce examples and updates per expert, which is precisely why the 2017 paper found diminishing returns and eventual degradation at extreme sparsity. MoE improves capacity per compute; it does not abolish sample complexity.
2. **Guaranteed knowledge partitioning.** A router may balance traffic without learning cleanly interpretable specialties.
3. **Guaranteed generalization.** Claims that MoE inherently prevents overfitting are too strong. Conditional parameters can help fit heterogeneous data, but sparsity can also create undertrained experts, brittle routes, and memorization.
4. **Linear wall-clock scaling.** Communication and memory bandwidth can dominate.
5. **Dense-size intelligence.** Total parameter count alone does not determine reasoning depth or benchmark performance.

This corrects the strongest version of the “data wall” argument. Finite high-quality data is a real constraint, and dense models make every token update every block. But Chinchilla's compute-optimal ratios do not establish a hard planetary parameter ceiling, and MoE does not make data requirements disappear. Repeated data, better curation, multimodal corpora, synthetic data, retrieval, distillation, and changed training objectives all alter the calculation. MoE is best understood as a response to the **compute-capacity coupling**, not as a proof that dense scaling is literally impossible.

#### 9. Why K3 still matters

With those corrections, the conclusion becomes stronger rather than weaker. K3 matters not because “2.78T” is a magical intelligence threshold, but because it represents the mature form of the architecture Shazeer et al. made practical:

- a large conditional parameter bank;
- a much smaller active path;
- routing and balancing engineered as first-class components;
- expert parallelism across a distributed system;
- quantization to control the enormous storage bill;
- efficient attention to prevent long context from overwhelming the savings;
- cheap enough per-token execution to support large-scale distillation and RL rollouts.

The 2017 paper's stated ambition was to train a trillion-parameter model on a trillion-word corpus. It did not reach that target, but it established the mechanism by which later systems could. The historical line is therefore:

**Jacobs/Jordan mixtures (1991-1994) → conditional-computation research (2013-2015) → Shazeer's sparsely-gated MoE (2017) → Transformer MoEs (2020 onward) → trillion-parameter sparse frontier models.**

#### 10. The one-paragraph version

**Mixture-of-Experts is not a loophole that turns 104B active parameters into a 2.78T dense computation. It is a conditional-computation architecture that stores a vast bank of expert weights and evaluates only the routes selected for each token.** Shazeer et al. (2017) made this practical with noisy top-k gating, auxiliary importance/load losses, and a distributed design that preserved large per-expert batches. Their experiments reached 137B parameters in the LSTM era, improved language modeling and translation at matched or lower compute, and exposed the limitations that still govern MoE: expert collapse, shrinking batches, communication cost, undertrained experts, and diminishing returns at excessive sparsity. K3's significance is that this once-experimental layer has become a complete scaling strategy. Its 2.78T total parameters describe conditional capacity; its 104.2B active parameters describe the route used for a token; neither number should replace the other. The future of sparse scaling depends not on parameter headlines alone, but on routing quality, data per expert, memory, interconnect, and measured capability per unit of compute.

## BOOK XXVI — THE CONCORDANCE: KEY CONCEPTS GLOSSARY

- **Artificial Neuron:** Simplified mathematical model of biological neurons. McCulloch-Pitts (1943), Perceptron (1958).
- **Backpropagation:** Algorithm for training multi-layer neural networks by propagating errors backward.
- **Attention Mechanism:** Method for focusing on relevant parts of input during processing.
- **Transformer:** Architecture based entirely on attention, no recurrence.
- **Self-Attention:** Attention within a single sequence—words attending to other words.
- **Multi-Head Attention:** Multiple attention heads learning different patterns.
- **Positional Encoding:** Adding positional information to token embeddings.
- **Word Embedding:** Dense vector representation of words (Word2Vec).
- **LSTM:** Recurrent neural network with memory cells for long-range dependencies.
- **GRU:** Simplified version of LSTM.
- **Seq2Seq:** Encoder-decoder model for sequence transformation.
- **Masked Language Modeling:** Predicting masked words in text (BERT).
- **Next Token Prediction:** Predicting the next word in text (GPT).
- **Few-Shot Learning:** Learning new tasks from a few examples in context.
- **In-Context Learning:** Learning from examples within the context window.
- **Reinforcement Learning:** Learning through trial and error and rewards.
- **Chain of Thought:** Explicit reasoning steps before answering.
- **Mixture-of-Experts:** Activating only relevant model parameters per token.
- **MoE:** A conditionally computed layer in which a router selects a small subset of expert networks for each token. It separates total parameter capacity from the active path, but still incurs storage, routing, communication, and per-expert data costs. K3 is described here as 2.78T total / 104.2B active.
- **Sparsity / Routing Ratio:** Report the denominator explicitly. For the K3 figures used here, `896/16 = 56×` is the routed-expert selection ratio, while `2.78T/104.2B ≈ 26.7×` is the whole-model total-to-active ratio. They are not interchangeable.
- **Dense-Equivalent:** An informal, benchmark-dependent comparison—not a universal formula. An MoE should be compared with dense models at matched training compute, inference compute, memory, latency, and measured quality; `√(active × total)` is at most a heuristic and not a scaling law.
- **Load Balancing / Quantile Balancing:** Ensuring every expert gets traffic so no parameters are dead. The difference between a real 2.78T model and a fake one.
- **Data Wall:** The finite supply of high-quality human-generated training data. Estimates are uncertain and do not imply a hard 2.8-4T dense-parameter ceiling; repeated data, multimodal and synthetic corpora, retrieval, and changed objectives alter the limit. MoE improves capacity per compute but does not eliminate sample complexity.
- **SLM (Small Language Model):** A language model small enough to train and run on consumer hardware — roughly 1k to 3B parameters. Not a shrunken LLM; a *component* (router, classifier, titler, summarizer) and a research instrument. Its ceiling is a **knowledge** ceiling, not a reasoning one (cf. BDH-CQ).
- **Latent Reasoning:** Performing multi-step computation by repeatedly transforming a continuous hidden state and decoding only the final answer — no intermediate tokens. Removes the discrete vocabulary bottleneck, lets one state hold several hypotheses at once, and decouples reasoning depth from context length and token cost. Contrast **chain-of-thought**, which is serial, verbalized, and priced per token (BDH-CQ, Coconut, recurrent-depth models).
- **BDH (Dragon Hatchling):** Pathway's post-Transformer architecture — a scale-free graph of locally-interacting neuron particles with sparse positive activations (~5% active), **no KV cache**, and working memory carried by Hebbian synaptic plasticity. Matches GPT-2-class performance at 10M-1B parameters and is interpretable (monosemantic) by design.
- **BDH-CQ:** A 150M-parameter BDH reasoning system that ingests demonstrations into recurrent memory `S_t` and solves the query by iterating a latent workspace `H_r`. **29.5% pass@2 on public ARC-AGI-1 at a computed $0.0007/task** — a state-of-the-art point on the benchmark's cost-accuracy Pareto frontier.
- **Cost-Accuracy Pareto Frontier:** The curve of "best score achievable at each price point." A system *breaks through* it when no existing system matches its accuracy at equal or lower cost — the axis on which small models can beat frontier models outright.
- **Reasoning Effort (latent):** A dial selecting how many latent iterations to run at inference (BDH-CQ: LOW 21% / MEDIUM 27% / HIGH 29.5% pass@2). The latent analogue of GPT-5's Instant/Thinking router and Claude's adaptive thinking.
- **Transductive vs. In-Context ARC Solving:** Transductive solvers (HRM, TRM) run backward passes on augmented *evaluation* puzzles with learned per-puzzle embeddings before predicting — costing ~$1.50/task. In-context solvers (BDH-CQ) write the task into memory through demonstrations with **no inference-time parameter updates**.
- **ARC-AGI:** Chollet's Abstraction and Reasoning Corpus — sparse demonstrations of a novel grid transformation, exact outputs required. Measures **skill-acquisition efficiency** rather than stored knowledge, which is why a 150M model can compete on it.
- **Over-training:** Deliberately training far past Chinchilla-optimal (300-2,700 tokens/param) to buy a permanently smaller, cheaper model. The economic engine of SLMs.
- **Knowledge Distillation:** Training a small student on a large teacher's outputs (Hinton 2015). See DistillSupra-0.2M (10× compression) and K3's MOPD.
- **Tokens-per-Parameter:** The Chinchilla ratio (~20 optimal). Supra2-100M uses 300; TinyLlama ~2,700.
- **Model Routing:** Using a tiny model to decide whether a request is handled locally by an SLM or escalated to a frontier model (Supra-Router-51M).
- **Gated DeltaNet:** Linear-recurrence layer used in hybrid small models (SupraBrain-50M), cousin of K3's KDA.
- **Muon Optimizer:** Newton-Schulz orthogonalization for 2D weight matrices — used at 2.78T (K3 Per-Head Muon) and at 50M (SupraBrain).
- **WSD Schedule:** Warmup-Stable-Decay learning rate schedule, standard in modern SLM pretraining.
- **FineWeb-Edu:** Filtered educational CommonCrawl derivative; the default SLM training corpus.
- **Scaling Law:** Predictable performance improvement with scale.
- **Context Window:** Maximum tokens the model can process at once.
- **Multimodal:** Processing multiple data types (text, image, video).
- **Agentic AI:** Models that act, use tools, and reason in the world.
- **RLHF:** Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback.
- **Constitutional AI:** Training for safety using AI-generated critiques.
- **Attention Residuals:** Attention across network depth (not just sequence).
- **KDA (Kimi Delta Attention):** Linear attention with bounded decay.
- **Flash Attention:** Hardware-efficient attention implementation.
- **Quantization-Aware Training:** Training with quantization in mind.
- **Speculative Decoding:** Using a draft model to accelerate inference.
- **MTP (Multi-Token Prediction):** Predicting multiple tokens per step.
- **Virtual Pipeline:** Efficient pipeline parallelism with virtual stages.
- **Expert Parallelism:** Distributing experts across devices.
- **Data Parallelism:** Replicating model across devices, splitting data.
- **Pipeline Parallelism:** Splitting model across devices layer by layer.
- **Context Parallelism:** Splitting sequence across devices for long contexts.
- **GQA (Grouped-Query Attention):** Sharing key-value heads to reduce memory.
- **NoPE:** No Positional Encoding—position handled by recurrence.
- **RoPE:** Rotary Positional Embedding.
- **YaRN:** Efficient context window extension.
- **Transformers.js:** JavaScript library (Xenova, 2023) that runs 🤗 Transformers via ONNX Runtime (WASM/WebGPU) 100% locally — `pipeline(task, model, { device: 'webgpu', dtype: 'q4' })` in browser/Node/Deno/Bun.
- **ONNX Runtime:** Open Neural Network Exchange runtime — compiles models to optimized graphs with hardware backends (WASM, WebGPU, CUDA). v4 rewrote WebGPU backend in C++ for 4× speedups.
- **WebGPU:** Successor to WebGL — low-level GPU compute API for the web (~70% global support in late 2024, 100% via fallback). Enables `device: 'webgpu'` in Transformers.js v3/v4.
- **WASM (WebAssembly):** Portable bytecode for near-native CPU speed in browsers — initial backend for Transformers.js v1/v2 before WebGPU.
- **Pipeline API:** High-level task API (`pipeline('automatic-speech-recognition', 'onnx-community/whisper-tiny.en')`) mirroring Python `transformers`.
- **Quantization dtype:** Per-model precision — `fp32`/`fp16`/`q8`/`q4`/`q1`/`bnb4` etc. introduced in v3, expanded to `q1f16`/`q2` in v4.1.
- **ModelRegistry:** v4 production API for pre-flight asset inspection (`getPipelineAssets()`) before download.
- **Contrib Operators:** Custom ONNX ops `MultiHeadAttention`/`GroupQueryAttention`/`MatMulNBits`/`QMoE` powering v4 MoE/Mamba/MLA models.
- **Constitutional AI (Anthropic):** Training via AI self-critique against a “constitution” (Claude 1→Opus 5) — reduces human labeling for harmlessness.
- **GPT Router (GPT-5):** Unified system that routes prompts between fast “Instant” and slower “Thinking” sub-models (Aug 7 2025).
- **Computer Use:** Model operating a desktop (mouse/keyboard/browser) — Claude 3.5 Sonnet Oct 2024 → GPT-5.4 Mar 2026 mainline.
- **Hybrid Reasoning (Claude 3.7):** Standard vs Extended Thinking slider (Feb 2025).
- **Agent Teams / Swarm:** Parallel sub-agents — Anthropic Agent Teams (Opus 4.6 Feb 2026), Kimi Agent Swarm (K2.5), GPT-5.6 multi-agent (Jul 2026).
- **GPT-5.6 Sol / Terra / Luna:** OpenAI frontier tiers (Jun 27 2026 preview, Aug 6 GA) — Sol optimized Xenova’s Kimi K3 graph 331→22 dispatches.
- **Kernels Library (HF):** Hugging Face library of fused kernels (KDA/MLA/MoE WebGPU) — used in Jul 24 2026 Kimi K3 optimization (65→406 tok/s, 1e-7 diff).
- **Dispatch:** One GPU kernel launch — fusion reduces 331 → 22 per token in Kimi K3 optimization.
- **Hopfield Network (1982):** Energy `E=-½Σw_ij s_i s_j` associative memory; modern version ≡ attention (Ramsauer 2020).
- **Boltzmann Machine / RBM (1985/86):** Stochastic Hopfield, `p∝exp(-E/kT)`; bipartite RBM enables layer-wise EBM pre-training.
- **Energy-Based Model (EBM, LeCun 2006):** Learn low energy for correct pairs, high for incorrect; unifies Hopfield/Boltzmann/diffusion score/JEPA.
- **VAE (Kingma & Welling, 2013/14):** Encoder `q(z|x)=N(μ,σ²)` + reparameterization `z=μ+σ·ε`; ELBO training; VQ-VAE backbone for latent diffusion.
- **GAN (Goodfellow 2014):** `min_G max_D` game; DCGAN/WGAN/CycleGAN/StyleGAN family; fast 1-step but mode collapse.
- **Neural Style Transfer (Gatys 2015):** Gram matrix `G^l` encodes style; first viral AI art.
- **Diffusion / DDPM (Sohl-Dickstein 2015 → Ho 2020):** Forward noise `x_t=√(1-β_t)x_{t-1}+√β ε`, learn reverse denoise `ε_θ`; Stable Diffusion = latent diffusion + VAE; Sora = space-time diffusion.
- **BatchNorm (Ioffe 2015):** Batch `x̂=(x-μ)/σ`, then `γx̂+β`; enables deep training.
- **ResNet (He 2015):** Skip `y=F(x)+x`; proven 152 layers; Transformer’s `Add & Norm`.
- **Dropout (Hinton 2012):** Randomly drop `p=0.5` neurons/train step — ensemble regularization.
- **CUDA / cuDNN / Tensor Cores (NVIDIA 2007/2014/2017):** GPGPU platform; 20-50× over CPU; why AlexNet scaled.
- **PyTorch / TensorFlow / TPU:** Autograd frameworks (TF Nov 2015 static, PyTorch Oct 2016 dynamic `autograd`); Google TPU ASICs (2016) for matmuls — hardware-software loop.
- **RAG (Lewis et al. 2020):** `p(y|x)= Σ_z p_η(z|x) p_θ(y|x,z)` (DPR + BART); RAG-Sequence vs Token; hot-swap index = no retrain.
- **JEPA (LeCun 2022):** Joint Embedding Predictive Architecture — predict `emb_y` from `emb_x` in latent space; I-JEPA/V-JEPA/V-JEPA 2; world-model alternative to LLM token prediction.
- **World Model / Neuro-Symbolic:** Hybrid net + symbolic/predictive model (AlphaGeometry 2024); JEPA as energy-based world model for physical reasoning.
- **Roblox AI Girlfriend Niche (2020-2022, community oral history — raw-code era):** COVID-era Roblox NPCs — *Phase 1 (2020-2022): pure raw Lua* (pattern tables / Markov chains / state flags, **no API, way before ChatGPT known**, more consistently coherent than Cleverbot by handcraft) in multiplayer hangouts; *Phase 2 (late 2022+): lazy API* (`HttpService → ChatGPT/OpenAI`) after ChatGPT — “what AI soon does.” Taught teen devs raw AI coding, then API outsourcing — forgotten grassroots precursor to modern AI companions/LLM NPCs.
- **DQN (Mnih 2013/15):** Deep Q-Network — CNN `Q(s,a)` from raw pixels + experience replay + target net; human-level Atari 29/49.
- **AlphaGo (2016):** MCTS + policy/value nets, beat Lee Sedol 4-1; **AlphaZero (2017):** self-play zero human, single ResNet `(p,v)` + MCTS masters chess/Go/Shogi — proved RL alone discovers superhuman play.
- **PPO (Schulman 2017):** Proximal Policy Optimization — on-policy RL used for InstructGPT/RLHF.
- **FlashAttention (Dao 2022):** IO-aware tiled exact attention, `O(Nd)` HBM vs `O(N²)`, 2-4× speedup, O(N) memory — every long-context model uses it (v2 2023, v3 2024 Hopper).
- **Mamba (Gu & Dao 2023):** Selective SSM `h'=Ah+Bx, y=Ch` where A/B/C input-dependent → linear O(N), constant state; sub-quadratic challenger pre-KDA.
- **Ring Attention (Liu 2023):** Blockwise attention sharded in a ring across devices + KV overlap → train 1M without OOM.
- **llama.cpp (Gerganov Mar 2023):** Pure C++ `ggml` inference, runs LLaMA on MacBook/Pi/CPU; MIT.
- **GGUF (Aug 2023):** Single-file quantized format for llama.cpp (`Q4_K_M` ~3.9 bits, 7B→3.8GB, ~97% FP16); successor to GGML; ~70% of HF community releases.
- **Ollama (Jul 2023):** “Docker for LLMs” (Go over llama.cpp) — `ollama run llama3`, OpenAI-compatible `localhost:11434`, `Modelfile`; 10-20% overhead but standard DX.
- **ViT (Dosovitskiy 2021):** 16×16 patches + pure Transformer beats CNNs — paradigm shift for vision (→ MoonViT-V2 in K3).
- **CLIP (Radford 2021):** Contrastive 400M image-text (`max cosine` true pairs) — bridge text↔vision (→ DALL-E/SD/GPT-4V/K3).
- **LoRA (Hu 2021):** `ΔW=BA, B(d×r)A(r×k), r<<d`, freeze `W₀`, train 0.1% params; merge at inference.
- **GPTQ (Frantar 2022):** Hessian `H=2XXᵀ` greedy 4-bit quantization; **AWQ (Lin 2023):** activation saliency scaling — both make q4 ~lossless.
- **QLoRA (Dettmers 2023):** NF4 4-bit base + double quant + paged optimizer + LoRA → 65B fine-tune on one 48GB GPU.
- **InstructGPT (Ouyang Mar 2022):** SFT (13k demos) + RM (33k ranks) + PPO (KL penalty) — exact recipe scaled to ChatGPT.
- **Chain-of-Thought / CoT (Wei Dec 2022):** “Let’s think step by step” + 8 exemplars → GPT-3 GSM8K 17%→58% without weight update.

- **R-CNN / Fast / Faster R-CNN:** region proposal + CNN classification (2014-15); Faster's Region Proposal Network made detection end-to-end (§VIII).
- **YOLO (Redmon 2016):** one-stage detection — grid + regression, 45-155 fps; spawned the YOLO ecosystem (§VIII).

- **Mask R-CNN (2017):** Faster R-CNN + mask branch — instance segmentation (§VIII).
- **SAM (Kirillov 2023):** Segment Anything — promptable segmentation foundation model; SA-1B (11M images, 1.1B masks) (§VIII).
- **NeRF (Mildenhall 2020) / 3D Gaussian Splatting (2023):** neural radiance fields → real-time explicit 3D Gaussians; photoreal novel-view synthesis (§VIII).
- **Contrastive loss / InfoNCE (van den Oord 2018):** pull positive pairs together, negatives apart — the engine of CLIP, SimCLR, DINO, ImageBind (§IX).
- **Adam / AdamW (Kingma-Ba 2014; Loshchilov-Hutter 2019):** per-parameter adaptive moments + decoupled weight decay — the default optimizer of the Transformer era (§IX).
- **Muon (Jordan 2024):** SGD-momentum + Newton-Schulz orthogonalization of matrix updates; ~2× LLM training speedup; used by Kimi K2/K3 and SupraBrain (§IX).
- **WSD schedule (MiniCPM 2024):** Warmup-Stable-Decay LR — train at peak indefinitely, branch checkpoints, decay the last 10-25%; used by Llama 3, Gemma 2, Supra2 (§IX).
- **Warmup + cosine decay:** the 2018-2023 default LR recipe (GPT-3, LLaMA); warmup dates to Vaswani 2017's `d^-0.5·min(step^-0.5, step·warmup^-1.5)` (§IX).
- **RLHF (InstructGPT 2022):** SFT → reward model (Bradley-Terry 1952) → PPO with KL penalty; the recipe behind ChatGPT and Claude (§X).
- **DPO (Rafailov 2023):** Direct Preference Optimization — closed-form RLHF without reward model or RL loop; "your LM is secretly a reward model" (§X).
- **GRPO (Shao 2024):** Group Relative Policy Optimization — no critic; group-mean baseline; the RL engine of DeepSeek-R1 and 2025 open reasoning models (§X, §XI).
- **RLVR:** RL from Verifiable Rewards — reward correctness (math answers, passing tests) instead of a learned reward model (§X, §XI).
- **Logic Theorist (Newell & Simon 1956):** the first AI reasoning program — heuristic search over Principia Mathematica proofs; where reasoning started (§XI).
- **GPS / STRIPS / MYCIN / Cyc:** symbolic reasoning lineage — means-ends analysis (1959), planning (1971), expert systems (1976), common-sense KB (1984-); died of the knowledge bottleneck (§XI).
- **Scratchpads (Nye 2021):** training models to emit intermediate computation — the direct predecessor of CoT (§XI).
- **Chain-of-Thought (Wei 2022):** "Let's think step by step" elicits reasoning without weight updates; GPT-3 GSM8K 17%→58% (§XI).
- **Self-Consistency / Least-to-Most / PAL / ToT / ReAct / STaR:** the prompting-era toolkit — sample-and-vote (2022), decomposition (2022), code execution (2022), tree search (2023), think-act loops (2022), self-taught rationales (2022) (§XI).
- **Test-time compute (Snell 2024):** spending inference FLOPs (more thinking/sampling/search) as a scaling axis; smaller model + optimized TTC beats 14× larger (§XI).
- **Reasoning model (o1 2024 → R1/K1.5 2025):** an LLM RL-trained to emit a long internal chain of thought before answering; test-time compute as a dial (§XI).
- **"Aha moment" (R1-Zero 2025):** pure-RL model spontaneously learns self-verification/backtracking mid-training without human design (§XI).
- **Multimodal deep learning (Ngiam 2011):** joint audio+video representations — the field's origin (§XVIII).
- **Flamingo (2022) / BLIP-2 Q-Former (2023) / LLaVA (2023):** the frozen-bridge era — frozen vision encoder + frozen LLM + learned connector; visual instruction tuning (§XVIII).
- **ImageBind (Meta 2023):** one contrastive embedding space for six modalities (image/video, text, audio, depth, thermal, IMU) (§XVIII).
